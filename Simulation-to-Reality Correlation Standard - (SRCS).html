Simulation-to-Reality Correlation Standard - (SRCS)

OOF™ Origin Open Foundation™

Independent Methodological Authority


Simulation-to-Reality Correlation Standard (SRCS) defines the governance
conditions under which materially relevant simulated conditions, behaviors,
states, relationships, events, transitions, and outputs are systematically
compared with corresponding Operational Reality in order to establish where
meaningful correlation exists, where material deviation occurs, how alignment
varies across conditions and dimensions, and what uncertainty and limitations
bound any resulting Simulation-to-Reality Correlation claim.

SRCS establishes the governed relationship between:

what the simulation produces

and:

what corresponding reality demonstrates.

It does not assume that similarity establishes equivalence.

It does not assume that one successful comparison establishes general
correspondence.

It does not convert observed agreement into Validation State.

Instead, SRCS provides the methodological correlation layer through which
similarities and differences between Simulation and Operational Reality remain
explicit, bounded, condition-specific, traceable, and available for subsequent
validation, reliance, recalibration, or simulation lifecycle governance.

Foundational Distinction

SIMULOS® establishes:

Simulation Similarity ≠ Reality
Equivalence

A simulation may resemble Operational Reality closely in one dimension while
diverging materially in another.

It may correlate strongly: under nominal
conditions,

and weakly: near boundaries, during transitions, under stress, in rare events,
or when multiple conditions interact.

Therefore Simulation-to-Reality Correlation must remain multidimensional,
contextual, and bounded.

Purpose

The purpose of SRCS is to prevent simulation programs from moving directly
from:

“The simulation produced this.”

to:

“Reality behaves this way.”

without a governed correlation process between those statements.

SRCS exists because: a realistic-looking output may still be materially wrong,
aggregate agreement may conceal local deviation, one accurate subsystem may
coexist with another poorly represented subsystem, correct nominal behavior
may coexist with incorrect failure behavior, apparent statistical similarity
may conceal different causal or dynamic structures, correlation may
deteriorate over time, reality itself may change, and simulation agreement may
depend upon conditions narrower than the resulting claim implies.

Operational Space

SRCS governs: Simulation-to-Reality comparison, comparison-object identity,
reality reference identity, correlation target definition, comparison
dimension selection, correlation criteria, correspondence thresholds,
behavioral correspondence, state correspondence, event correspondence,
transition correspondence, output correspondence, distributional
correspondence, temporal correspondence, spatial correspondence, sequence
correspondence, interaction correspondence, boundary correspondence,
stress-condition correspondence, recovery correspondence, comparative context,
correlation strength, conditional correlation, partial correlation, local
correlation, global correlation, correlation asymmetry, deviation
identification, deviation magnitude, deviation direction, deviation
localization, deviation persistence, deviation pattern, systematic deviation,
random deviation, reality alignment, alignment boundaries, alignment
deterioration, alignment improvement, correlation uncertainty, correlation
limitations, correlation scope, correlation conditions, correlation change,
correlation traceability, and Simulation-to-Reality Correlation records.

Operational Scope

SRCS applies wherever a simulation explicitly or implicitly claims to
represent aspects of reality sufficiently for comparison.

It may apply across: aerospace, spacecraft and mission simulation, autonomous
systems, robotics, AI systems, digital twins, industrial processes,
manufacturing systems, transportation, infrastructure, energy systems,
environmental simulation, scientific models, financial systems, healthcare
systems, human-system interaction, multi-agent systems, cyber-physical
systems, operational planning, and future simulation environments.

SRCS is technology-neutral.

It does not prescribe one mathematical correlation coefficient, statistical
method, physical measurement system, calibration method, or validation
technique.

The governed question remains:

Does the simulation correspond to reality sufficiently for the specific
relationship being claimed?

Correlation Object

A Correlation Object is the specific simulation behavior, state, output,
event, relationship, transition, or other simulation result being compared
with a corresponding reality reference.

Examples may conceptually include:

Simulated trajectory ↔ observed trajectory

Simulated thermal behavior ↔ measured
thermal behavior

Simulated agent response ↔ observed agent
response

Simulated failure progression ↔
operational failure progression

Simulated demand pattern ↔ actual demand
pattern

Simulated system transition ↔ observed
system transition

SRCS requires clarity about what exactly is being correlated.

Reality Reference

A Simulation-to-Reality Correlation requires a corresponding Reality
Reference.

A Reality Reference may consist of: operational measurements, sensor records,
physical observations, mission telemetry, field behavior, experimental
observations, operational incidents, verified historical records, or another
sufficiently identified representation of Operational Reality.

SRCS does not govern Operational Reality itself.

ORA™ may provide or govern the relevant Operational Reality basis.

SRCS governs the relationship between that basis and the simulation.

Comparable Conditions Principle

Simulation and reality should not be compared as if they were equivalent where
materially relevant conditions differ.

Possible comparison dimensions may include: object version, environment, input
conditions, initial state, operating state, system configuration, population,
timing, scenario, boundary conditions, stress level, and dependencies.

SIMULOS® establishes:

Correlation requires sufficient comparability of the conditions being
compared.

Correlation Target

A Correlation Target defines what correspondence is being examined.

For example:

Trajectory correspondence

is different from:

energy-consumption correspondence

which is different from:

failure-sequence correspondence.

Strong correlation in one target must not be generalized automatically to
another.

No Correlation Transfer Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Correlation demonstrated for one behavior, condition, subsystem, environment,
population, or scenario does not automatically transfer to materially
different ones.

Correlation Criteria

Every material Simulation-to-Reality Correlation assessment should operate
against sufficiently defined criteria.

Correlation Criteria may concern: allowable deviation, behavioral pattern,
directionality, ordering, timing, distribution, state transition, physical
tolerance, response shape, event occurrence, frequency, magnitude, or another
simulation-specific relationship.

Criteria should be defined sufficiently to distinguish:

acceptable correspondence

from:

material deviation. Correlation Criteria ≠ Validation Criteria

Correlation Criteria answer:

How similar or aligned are the Simulation and Reality with respect to this
defined relationship?

VALIDOS® Validation Criteria may later answer:

Is the resulting correlation evidence sufficient to support a particular
Validation Determination?

These are related but distinct governance questions.

Direct Correlation

Direct Correlation occurs where a simulated quantity or behavior can be
compared directly with a corresponding observed quantity or behavior.

Conceptually:

Simulated X ↔ Observed X

Direct correspondence may be strong, weak, conditional, or absent.

Indirect Correlation

Some simulated properties cannot be observed directly.

Correlation may therefore depend upon: proxies, derived observations,
intermediate measurements, aggregate behavior, or indirect operational
signals.

Where correlation is indirect, the intermediate relationship should remain
explicit.

Proxy Correlation Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Agreement with a proxy does not automatically establish equivalence with the
underlying reality the proxy represents.

Behavioral Correlation

Behavioral Correlation assesses whether simulated behavior sufficiently
corresponds to observed behavior.

This may include: response to inputs, adaptation, recovery, escalation,
coordination, failure, control behavior, or agent interaction.

State Correlation

State Correlation assesses whether the simulated system reaches or occupies
states corresponding sufficiently to observed states.

Transition Correlation

Transition Correlation assesses whether the simulation reproduces materially
relevant movement between states.

This is important because:

Correct State A + Correct State B ≠ Correct A→B Transition Temporal
Correlation

Temporal Correlation concerns whether simulation events occur: at comparable
times, with comparable delays, in comparable sequences, or across comparable
durations.

A simulation may reproduce an event but still materially misrepresent when it
occurs.

Spatial Correlation

Spatial Correlation concerns whether simulated positions, distributions,
paths, fields, or spatial relationships correspond sufficiently to reality.

Distributional Correlation

Aggregate values may agree while distributions differ.

For example:

same mean

may coexist with:

different variance, tails, clustering, or multimodality.

SRCS therefore does not allow aggregate similarity automatically to establish
distributional correlation.

Sequence Correlation

Systems may generate the correct events in the wrong order.

Where order matters, SRCS evaluates:

Event Identity + Event Sequence

rather than event presence alone.

Interaction Correlation

Interaction Correlation concerns whether simulated relationships between:
agents, subsystems, humans, environments, dependencies, or other actors

correspond sufficiently to observed relationships.

Boundary Correlation

A simulation may correlate well in the center of the operating envelope but
poorly near limits.

SRCS therefore supports correlation assessment across:

Nominal Region

Boundary Region

Threshold Region

and where relevant:

Beyond-Nominal Region.

Stress Correlation

Where Operational Reality provides evidence from degraded or stressed
conditions, SRCS may compare:

Simulated Stress Behavior

with:

Observed Stress Behavior.

Strong nominal correlation must not substitute for absent stress correlation
where stress behavior materially matters.

Recovery Correlation

Recovery behavior may require separate correlation.

A model may accurately reproduce failure onset while poorly reproducing:
fallback, restoration, adaptation, restart, or recovery duration.

Local Correlation

Local Correlation exists where Simulation and Reality correspond sufficiently
within a bounded region of: state, environment, population, configuration,
parameter space, or scenario conditions.

Global Correlation

Global Correlation represents broader correspondence across the declared
Simulation Scope.

Global correlation should not be claimed by extrapolating from narrow local
correlation without an adequate basis.

No Local-to-Global Inflation Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Strong local Simulation-to-Reality Correlation does not establish global
correlation across unexamined Scenario Space.

Conditional Correlation

Correlation may hold only under explicit conditions.

For example:

Strong correlation under nominal thermal
conditions

but:

weak correlation under rapid thermal transition.

The resulting correlation record should preserve those conditions.

Partial Correlation

Different aspects of the same simulation may correlate differently.

For example:

Position — Strong

Velocity — Strong

Energy Consumption — Moderate

Recovery Timing — Weak

The simulation should not be given one undifferentiated correlation label if
material dimensions differ.

Correlation Profile

A Simulation-to-Reality Correlation Profile may represent multidimensional
correspondence across relevant dimensions.

This is often methodologically stronger than one global score.

No Single-Score Requirement

SIMULOS® does not require correlation to be represented through one numerical
metric.

A single score may conceal: strong and weak dimensions, local deviation,
conditional relationships, failure-region divergence, or uncertainty.

Where a numerical score is used, its meaning and limitations should remain
explicit.

Correlation Strength

Where useful, correlation may be classified as:

Strong

Moderate

Weak

Conditional

Partial

No Material Correlation

or:

Correlation Undetermined

provided the classification is tied to explicit criteria. Correlation Strength
≠ Validity

SIMULOS® establishes:

Strong Simulation-to-Reality Correlation does not itself establish that the
simulation is Validated for every intended use.

Correlation may become one component of a broader VALIDOS® validation record.
Deviation

A Simulation-to-Reality Deviation is a materially relevant difference between
simulated behavior and corresponding Operational Reality.

Deviation may concern: value, timing, direction, sequence, magnitude,
distribution, state, transition, interaction, failure, recovery, or another
governed property.

Deviation Is Not Automatically Failure

A deviation may be: expected, acceptable, immaterial, explainable,
model-induced, data-induced, environment-induced, reality-induced, or
materially problematic.

SRCS governs identification and characterization before drawing stronger
conclusions.

Deviation Magnitude

Deviation Magnitude describes how large the difference is relative to the
applicable comparison basis.

Magnitude should be interpreted together with materiality.

A numerically small deviation may be critical near a threshold.

A numerically larger deviation may be inconsequential elsewhere.

Deviation Direction

Deviation may systematically: overestimate, underestimate, anticipate, delay,
amplify, suppress, accelerate, or otherwise bias simulated behavior relative
to reality.

Direction can reveal patterns that aggregate absolute error may hide.

Systematic Deviation

A Systematic Deviation persists predictably across comparable conditions.

This may signal: model bias, incorrect parameterization, representation
weakness, input mismatch, missing interaction, or another simulation
limitation.

SRCS identifies the pattern.

The appropriate upstream SIMULOS® governance space determines what requires
correction.

Random Deviation

Some deviations may vary without stable direction.

Random deviation may still be material where its magnitude affects the
intended Simulation Purpose.

Localized Deviation

A simulation may correlate strongly overall while diverging materially in one
bounded region.

This creates a Localized Correlation Gap rather than necessarily a global
simulation failure.

Deviation Map

Where appropriate, SRCS may represent:

Scenario Space → Correlation Strength /
Deviation

This may reveal regions where the simulation remains strongly reality-aligned
and regions where it does not.

Deviation Attribution

Where possible, deviations should be connected to potential contributing
factors such as: Reality Representation, Simulation Assumptions, model
structure, environment configuration, parameters, inputs, transformation,
Scenario Coverage, execution conditions, output interpretation, or changed
Operational Reality.

Attribution should remain evidence-bounded.

No Automatic Cause Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Observed Simulation-to-Reality deviation identifies a difference; it does not
automatically establish why that difference exists.

Reality Alignment

Reality Alignment describes the degree to which the simulation continues to
correspond sufficiently to relevant Operational Reality across the declared
conditions.

Alignment may be:

Strong

Conditional

Partial

Weak

Misaligned

or:

Undetermined.

Reality Alignment Boundary

A simulation may have a clearly bounded region where alignment is sufficient.

Conceptually:

Aligned Region

↓

Transition Region

↓

Misaligned Region

Identifying this boundary may be more informative than assigning one global
realism label.

No Reality-Equivalence Claim

SIMULOS® establishes:

No degree of observed Simulation-to-Reality similarity should be represented
as complete Reality Equivalence unless such equivalence has been independently
and legitimately established for the exact governed claim.

For complex systems, such universal equivalence may often be impossible to
establish.

Correlation Uncertainty

A Simulation-to-Reality Correlation claim itself contains uncertainty.

Possible sources include: measurement uncertainty, operational observation
limitations, limited reality data, sparse comparison conditions, input
uncertainty, scenario mismatch, temporal mismatch, stochastic behavior,
environment differences, system change, and uncertainty in correlation
methods.

Correlation Uncertainty Must Remain
Attached

A correlation statement should not imply greater certainty than its comparison
basis supports.

Conceptually:

Correlation Result + Correlation
Uncertainty + Conditions + Scope

should remain connected.

No Correlation Precision Inflation

A precisely calculated correlation metric does not imply equivalent certainty
in the reality relationship where the observational basis is limited.

Reality Observation Limitation

Operational Reality may itself be incompletely observable.

For example: internal system states may not be measurable, field observations
may be sparse, critical failures may be rare, measurements may be noisy,
mission data may be incomplete, or historical records may lack relevant
context.

SRCS preserves this limitation rather than treating reality observations as
perfect ground truth by default. Operational Reality ≠ Perfect Knowledge of
Reality

This distinction is important.

ORA™ may govern the best available Operational Reality representation.

SRCS then correlates the simulation against that governed reality basis.

The limitations of that basis should remain visible.

Correlation Coverage

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation should be assessed relative to the Scenario
Space actually represented by Operational Reality observations.

If correlation has been established only under:

Conditions A, B, and C

then the simulation should not claim established correlation under:

D, E, and F

without further basis.

Correlation Coverage Gap

A Correlation Coverage Gap exists where materially relevant Simulation
Scenario Space lacks sufficient corresponding Operational Reality evidence for
meaningful correlation assessment.

This may occur because: the event has never occurred operationally, the
condition cannot safely be reproduced, mission opportunities are limited,
observations are incomplete, or future systems do not yet exist operationally.

Uncorrelatable Scenario Space

Some Scenario Space may currently be impossible to correlate directly with
reality.

For example: catastrophic spacecraft combinations never observed, future
autonomy modes, novel environmental conditions, future infrastructure
configurations, or hypothetical emergency states.

The correct governance state may be:

Correlation Not Yet Establishable

rather than:

Correlation Assumed.

No Absence-of-Reality-Data Inference

SIMULOS® establishes:

Where corresponding Operational Reality evidence does not exist, correlation
must not be manufactured from simulation evidence alone.

Correlation Change

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation may change even if the simulation itself
does not.

Reality may evolve through: system modifications, environmental change,
population change, infrastructure change, operational behavior change,
software updates, mission changes, or new dependencies.

Correlation Drift

Correlation Drift occurs when previously established correspondence between
Simulation and Reality weakens or changes over time.

This may be caused by:

Simulation Change

Reality Change

or:

Both.

No Permanent Correlation Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

A previously established Simulation-to-Reality Correlation must not be assumed
permanent when the simulation or corresponding Operational Reality materially
changes.

Correlation Reassessment Trigger

Reassessment may be required when there is material change in: Simulation
Object, Reality Representation, Simulation Assumptions, model, environment,
parameters, inputs, scenarios, execution, output interpretation, operational
environment, object version, population, mission phase, dependency, or
available reality evidence.

Correlation Improvement

Correlation may improve after: model refinement, better Reality
Representation, improved parameters, better input data, new operational
observations, additional scenario coverage, or correction of systematic
deviation.

Improvement should be demonstrated, not presumed.

Correlation Deterioration

New Operational Reality evidence may reveal that previous correspondence was
overstated or limited.

SRCS must remain capable of producing a weaker correlation conclusion.

No Positive-Bias Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation governance must remain capable of
demonstrating weak correlation, partial correlation, material deviation, or
unresolved correspondence.

Simulation Calibration Boundary

Correlation findings may inform simulation calibration.

However:

SRCS does not own general model calibration.

Its role is to identify:

where and how Simulation and Reality correspond or diverge.

The appropriate model, parameter, input, representation, or environment
governance space may then address the cause.

No Calibration-to-Correlation Circularity

A simulation tuned using one reality dataset should not automatically be
represented as independently correlated against that same dataset without
disclosure.

SIMULOS® establishes:

Data used to calibrate the simulation and data used to demonstrate
Simulation-to-Reality Correlation should remain distinguishable where
independence materially affects the claim.

Correlation Independence

Where a stronger correlation claim requires independent reality evidence, that
independence should remain explicit.

This prevents:

fit

from being confused with:

independent correspondence.

Overfitting to Reality Samples

A simulation may reproduce observed cases closely while generalizing poorly to
new operational conditions.

SRCS therefore distinguishes:

Correlation with Known Observations

from:

Correlation across materially broader Operational Reality.

Reality Holdout Principle

Where appropriate, previously unused Operational Reality observations may
provide stronger evidence of generalizable correlation than observations used
to construct or calibrate the simulation.

SRCS does not prescribe a specific holdout method.

It preserves the methodological distinction.

Cross-Environment Correlation

Correlation in one environment must not automatically transfer to another.

Examples:

Earth Laboratory ≠ Orbital Environment

Dry Road ≠ Snow

Factory A ≠ Factory B

Population A ≠ Population B

Low-Traffic Environment ≠ Dense Multi-Agent Environment Cross-Version
Correlation

A correlation conclusion for one Simulation Object or real-system version
should not automatically apply to materially changed versions.

Correlation Across Mission Phases

For mission systems, correlation may differ across: launch, cruise, orbital
operations, docking, landing, autonomous recovery, safe mode, or other phases.

A global mission-level correlation statement may conceal weak phase-specific
correspondence.

Autonomous-System Correlation

Autonomous systems present a particularly important correlation problem.

Their simulated behavior may depend upon: perception, internal state, external
agents, communication, timing, memory, changing permissions, human
intervention, uncertainty, and adaptive decision paths.

SRCS therefore supports correlation not only of final outputs but of: state
transitions, decisions, timing, fallback, escalation, recovery, and
interaction patterns.

Space and Mission Correlation

Space systems create a particularly difficult Simulation-to-Reality
Correlation environment because: real mission observations are expensive, some
events occur only once, catastrophic states may never be directly observed
before deployment, space environments can be difficult to reproduce
physically, mission phases differ materially, telemetry can be incomplete, and
operational configurations evolve.

SRCS therefore does not require impossible reality equivalence.

Instead it provides a governed structure for distinguishing:

what has been correlated

from:

what remains extrapolated, simulated-only, uncertain, or operationally
unobserved.

This distinction can be critical where mission decisions depend heavily upon
simulation.

Autonomous Vehicle Correlation

An autonomous vehicle simulation may correlate strongly with reality for:
ordinary road following, standard braking, common pedestrian behavior, and
normal weather.

That does not establish equivalent correlation for: rare interactions,
construction zones, degraded sensors, unusual road markings, emergency vehicle
behavior, or compound edge conditions.

SRCS keeps the correlation boundary visible.

Digital Twin Correlation

A digital twin may continuously compare:

Simulated State

with:

Operational State.

SRCS may govern: alignment, deviation, temporal lag, drift, systematic
mismatch, and correlation deterioration.

A digital twin that ceases to correlate sufficiently with the physical system
should not continue to be represented as an equivalent operational
representation merely because the software remains active.

Governance Boundary

SRCS begins when governed Simulation Outputs or Simulation Behaviors exist and
corresponding Operational Reality evidence is sufficiently identifiable for
comparison.

SRCS ends when: the comparison target is identified, the corresponding reality
basis is established, relevant correlation criteria are applied, material
deviations are identified, Reality Alignment is assessed, correlation
uncertainty and limitations are preserved, and the Simulation-to-Reality
relationship is expressed as a bounded, traceable correlation record.

SRCS does not determine: Operational Reality itself, universal simulation
validity, general model integrity, final Validation Determination, deployment
authorization, operational safety, or unrestricted reliance.

Architectural Separation

SRCS must remain separated from four neighboring governance questions:

Reality Representation → How is reality represented inside the simulation?

Simulation Output Governance → What exactly did the simulation produce and
what may be inferred from it?

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation → How does that simulated result correspond
to actual Operational Reality?

Validation Governance → What formal validation conclusion may legitimately be
established from the complete validation record?

This separation is fundamental to SIMULOS®.

Canonical Principle

Simulation similarity is not reality equivalence.

Supporting Principle

A simulation should be trusted as representative of reality only to the extent
that the relevant relationship between simulated and observed reality has
actually been examined, bounded, and kept traceable to the conditions under
which that correspondence was established.

Foundational Principle

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation becomes methodologically legitimate only
when similarity and deviation are evaluated against an identifiable
Operational Reality basis, within explicit comparison conditions, using
governed criteria, while preserving the uncertainty and boundaries of the
resulting correlation claim.

Canonical Closing Statement

Simulation-to-Reality Correlation Standard establishes the governed bridge
between Simulation and Operational Reality within SIMULOS®. By governing the
identification of correlation targets, comparison conditions, correlation
criteria, behavioral and state correspondence, transitions, distributions,
timing, interactions, boundaries, deviations, Reality Alignment, Correlation
Coverage, uncertainty, drift, reassessment, and complete Simulation-to-Reality
traceability, SRCS prevents realistic appearance, numerical similarity,
nominal agreement, or isolated successful comparison from being mistaken for
Reality Equivalence. It enables simulations to state not merely that they
resemble reality, but precisely where, under which conditions, to what extent,
and with what uncertainty that correspondence has actually been
demonstrated—while preserving the architectural boundary through which ORA™
governs Operational Reality and VALIDOS® governs the validation conclusions
that may ultimately be drawn from the correlation record.
