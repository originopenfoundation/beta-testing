Understanding Tax-Relevant Event Timing

Real-world and digital events do not always occur at one simple,
universally agreed moment.

An event may begin at one time, continue across multiple
operational stages, depend on earlier actions, complete later,
and be represented differently across transactional, accounting,
platform, machine, or autonomous systems. Distributed systems may
record different timestamps for the same operational activity,
while autonomous systems may generate thousands of machine
actions within a single governed event.

Before taxation systems can evaluate such activity, the event
itself must therefore have a governed temporal representation.

The Tax-Relevant Event Timing Standard (TRETS) establishes that
representation.

TRETS governs the operational timing of the event before
jurisdiction-specific tax rules determine what legal tax
significance that timing may have.

Operational Event Time ≠ Legal Tax Point. What Is TRETS?

TRETS is the fifth Parent Standard within TREGA™ — Tax-Relevant
Event Governance Architecture™.

It receives a Governed Bounded Event from the Tax-Relevant Event
Boundary Standard (TREBS) and establishes the governed temporal
structure necessary for subsequent Event Location governance.

TRETS determines how the event is temporally situated by
governing its relevant operational time, start, completion,
duration, and temporal order.

Its principal transition is:

Governed Bounded Event

↓

Governed Temporally Situated Event

TRETS does not determine when tax legally becomes due, which tax
period applies, or what jurisdiction-specific taxation rules mean
for the event.

Canonical Definition

Tax-Relevant Event Timing Standard governs how a sufficiently
bounded real-world, economic, operational, transactional,
digital, autonomous, machine-generated, or value-affecting event
is temporally situated through governed identification of its
operational time, start, completion, duration, and temporal
order, while preserving the distinction between operational Event
Timing and jurisdiction-specific legal tax timing, including
Legal Tax Point and Tax Period Determination.

Why TRETS Exists

Modern operational systems produce timestamps constantly.

An order may be created at one moment, accepted later, fulfilled
across several stages, settled afterward, corrected subsequently,
and recorded differently by multiple systems.

A machine-generated event may have no single human action that
naturally represents its time.

A distributed digital service may execute across multiple systems
whose clocks, processing sequences, and records differ.

A commercial system may use invoice time.

A payment system may use settlement time.

A logistics system may use delivery time.

A platform may use transaction-confirmation time.

A jurisdiction may subsequently apply an entirely different legal
rule for determining the relevant tax point.

None of these timestamps should automatically become the governed
operational time of the event merely because a particular system
recorded them.

TRETS creates a methodology layer capable of establishing the
event's temporal reality independently from the legal tax rule
subsequently applied.

Event Location

TREBS establishes what structurally belongs to the event.

TRETS establishes how that bounded event is situated in
operational time.

TRELS subsequently establishes how the temporally situated event
is represented in operational location.

This ordering prevents later legal or jurisdictional assumptions
from silently determining the event's underlying operational
timing.

What TRETS Governs

TRETS governs the temporal representation of a sufficiently
bounded event, including: Event Time identification Event Start
Event Completion Event Duration Event Temporal Order multiple or
competing temporal signals distributed temporal representations
temporal ambiguity and uncertainty temporal coherence temporal
traceability machine-generated timing autonomous-system timing

The objective is not simply to attach a timestamp.

The objective is to establish a governed temporal representation
capable of being reconstructed, examined, and consumed by
subsequent governance and taxation systems.

TRETS does not determine: Legal Tax Point Tax Period Tax

These remain outside the governed space of TRETS.

Event Timing ≠ Tax Period Determination.

Operational Time and Legal Tax Time

One of the most important distinctions established by TRETS is
the separation between the operational temporal reality of an
event and the legal interpretation subsequently applied to that
event.

TRETS may establish that a governed event: began at Time A,
materially progressed through Times B and C, completed at Time D,
and occurred in a defined temporal sequence relative to other
governed events.

A jurisdiction-specific taxation system may subsequently
determine that the legally relevant tax point is Time B, Time D,
another legally defined moment, or a period derived according to
applicable law.

That legal determination does not retroactively redefine the
operational history of the event.

TRETS therefore preserves a stable governed temporal
representation while allowing different jurisdictions to apply
different legal tax interpretations.

Multiple Temporal Signals

Modern events frequently contain multiple legitimate temporal
signals.

Examples include:

transaction initiation time authorization time execution time
confirmation time shipment time delivery time settlement time
system-recording time machine-observation time ledger-recording
time

TRETS does not assume that one technical timestamp automatically
governs the entire event.

The relevant temporal structure must instead be established from
the governed event itself.

Timestamp ≠ Governed Event Time by Default.

Distributed and Machine-Generated Timing

Autonomous and distributed systems create additional temporal
complexity.

A single governed activity may involve: AI agents, APIs, robots,
distributed databases, cloud infrastructure, payment systems,
digital assets, machine-to-machine interactions, asynchronous
processing, geographically distributed execution.

Different components may record different times for different
parts of the same activity.

TRETS provides the governance structure necessary to preserve
these temporal signals while establishing a coherent operational
temporal representation of the governed event.

Machine Timestamp ≠ Event Time by Default.

Temporal Order

Event Timing also concerns relationships between moments within
the event.

A governed event may contain several operational stages whose
order matters even when their exact timestamps are uncertain or
disputed.

TRETS therefore governs temporal ordering independently from the
subsequent Event Relationship governance performed by TRERLS.

TRETS asks:

In what temporal order did the governed event and its relevant
temporal stages occur?

It does not determine the broader governed relationship between
separate events.

Real-System Use Case 1 — Cross-Border Digital Marketplace
Transaction

A customer places an order through a global marketplace at 23:58.

The platform confirms the transaction at 00:01.

Payment authorization occurs at 00:03.

The merchant accepts the order several hours later.

Goods are shipped the following day.

Delivery occurs three days later.

Financial settlement occurs another two days afterward.

Different systems therefore contain several potentially relevant
timestamps.

If one system timestamp were automatically treated as the event
time, the underlying operational event could be represented
differently depending on which database, platform, accounting
system, or jurisdiction examines it.

TRETS governs the event's temporal structure independently.

It identifies and preserves relevant temporal signals,
establishes Event Start, Event Completion, Duration, and Temporal
Order, and produces a traceable Governed Temporally Situated
Event.

Only afterward may different jurisdictions determine which
legally defined moment constitutes their applicable tax point or
tax period.

The operational timeline is governed once; its legal tax
interpretation may differ by jurisdiction. Real-System Use Case 2
— Autonomous Machine-to-Machine Economy

An autonomous industrial AI system detects demand, schedules
production, activates robotic equipment, purchases machine
capacity, consumes digital services, coordinates logistics, and
automatically initiates settlement.

Thousands of machine actions occur across distributed systems.

Individual machines generate timestamps in milliseconds.

Cloud services record different processing times.

The payment infrastructure records authorization and settlement
separately.

No human presses a button that naturally defines when the
economic event occurred.

Treating every machine timestamp as an independent event time
would create artificial fragmentation.

Treating the entire autonomous workflow as occurring at one
timestamp would destroy important temporal structure.

TRETS establishes a governed temporal representation capable of
distinguishing operationally relevant timing from technical
execution noise.

The resulting event can then proceed through the TREGA™ lifecycle
and ultimately be evaluated by jurisdiction-specific taxation
systems.

Machine execution time does not automatically define the governed
time of the event.

Canonical Boundary

TRETS governs the operational temporal representation of a
sufficiently bounded event, including Event Time identification,
Event Start, Event Completion, Event Duration, Event Temporal
Order, temporal ambiguity, competing temporal signals,
distributed timing, machine-generated timing, and temporal
traceability.

TRETS does not govern Event Formation, Event Boundary, Event
Location, Tax Jurisdiction, Event Participation, taxpayer
determination, value lifecycle, Event-to-Value Association, Event
Relationships, final Event Determination, Legal Tax Point, Tax
Period Determination, legal taxability, tax base, tax rate,
exemptions, deductions, tax liability, reporting obligations,
payment, collection, or enforcement.

TRETS begins with a Governed Bounded Event and ends with a
Governed Temporally Situated Event.

Canonical Closing Statement

TRETS establishes the governed temporal reality of an event
before jurisdiction-specific taxation systems interpret that
reality under law.

It prevents database timestamps, accounting dates, platform
records, payment timestamps, machine clocks, autonomous execution
sequences, and downstream taxation assumptions from silently
becoming authoritative representations of when the event
occurred.

TRETS governs when the event occurred operationally — not when
tax law decides the event becomes legally relevant.

TREGA™ governs the event. The jurisdiction governs the tax.
