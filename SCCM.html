Governed Space

Simulation Scenario Coverage

Canonical Definition

Scenario Coverage Module (SCCM) defines the governance framework for
determining, documenting, measuring where appropriate, challenging, and
maintaining whether the governed Scenario Set sufficiently represents the
materially relevant Scenario Space required by the Simulation Purpose.

SCCM governs the relationship between:

what could materially matter

and:

what the simulation program has actually selected for examination.

It integrates the governed outputs of Scenario Identification, Scenario
Variation, Scenario Edge-Case governance, and Scenario Stress-Condition
governance into a structured coverage basis capable of revealing represented
regions, insufficiently represented regions, excluded regions, unknown
regions, and material Scenario Coverage Gaps.

Its purpose is not to claim that every possible scenario has been simulated.

Its purpose is to make explicit:

what Scenario Space has been covered, what remains uncovered, why that
remaining gap matters, and what claims may legitimately be made from the
resulting scenario basis.

Operational Role

SCCM serves as the fifth and final internal governance space of the Simulation
Scenario Standard (SSS).

The preceding modules establish:

SCIM — Which scenarios materially belong in the simulation inquiry?

SCVM — Across which conditions must those scenarios vary?

SECM — Which edges, thresholds, transitions, sparse regions, and unusual
interactions require deliberate examination?

SSCM — Which materially demanding conditions must be stressed to expose
degradation, limits, failure, cascading effects, and recovery behavior?

SCCM asks:

Does the resulting governed Scenario Set sufficiently cover the materially
relevant Scenario Space for the Simulation Purpose, and where does meaningful
coverage remain absent, weak, uncertain, or unknown?

Scenario Space

A Scenario Space is the governed set of materially relevant conditions,
configurations, variations, interactions, edges, stresses, transitions,
sequences, and contextual states within which the Simulation Object may need
to be examined for the declared Simulation Purpose.

Scenario Space is not necessarily equivalent to:

all theoretically imaginable situations.

It is bounded by: Simulation Object, Simulation Purpose, Simulation Scope,
Reality Representation, Simulation Assumptions, Model & Environment, Inputs &
Data, Scenario Requirements, and materially relevant operational conditions.

Scenario Coverage

Scenario Coverage represents the extent to which the governed Scenario Set
addresses the materially relevant Scenario Space.

Coverage can concern: breadth, depth, boundary representation, interaction
representation, stress representation, temporal representation, configuration
representation, or another simulation-specific dimension.

Coverage Is Multidimensional

A single percentage may hide material weaknesses.

For example:

95% Scenario Coverage

has little meaning if it represents only:

95% of nominal operating combinations

while: critical transitions, edge cases, stress conditions, recovery states,
and rare high-consequence interactions

remain unexamined.

SIMULOS® therefore establishes:

Scenario Coverage should be interpreted across materially relevant dimensions
rather than reduced automatically to one aggregate number.

Coverage Requirement

A Coverage Requirement states what portion or type of Scenario Space must be
represented sufficiently for the Simulation Purpose.

A Coverage Requirement should remain connected to: purpose, scope,
consequence, uncertainty, system complexity, operational relevance, and
intended downstream reliance.

No Universal Coverage Threshold

SIMULOS® does not prescribe one universal percentage of sufficient Scenario
Coverage.

The appropriate level depends upon the governed simulation inquiry.

A low-consequence exploratory simulation may legitimately require different
coverage from: spacecraft autonomy, autonomous vehicles, critical
infrastructure, medical systems, industrial safety systems, or
high-consequence AI agents.

Coverage Depth

Coverage is not only about whether a condition appears once.

A region may require deeper examination through: additional variations,
repeated scenarios, higher resolution, more configurations, longer duration,
additional interactions, or more demanding stress levels.

SCCM therefore distinguishes:

Coverage Presence

from:

Coverage Depth.

Coverage Breadth

Coverage Breadth represents how widely the Scenario Set spans materially
relevant Scenario Space.

Broad coverage with insufficient depth may remain inadequate.

Deep examination of one narrow region may also remain inadequate.

Coverage Balance

SCCM may examine whether simulation effort is disproportionately concentrated
in convenient or nominal regions while difficult regions remain sparse.

This is a critical diagnostic function.

A simulation program can contain millions of runs and still possess poor
Scenario Coverage if those runs repeatedly examine nearly identical
conditions. Run Count ≠ Coverage

SIMULOS® establishes:

The number of simulation executions is not itself evidence of Scenario
Coverage.

Ten million executions concentrated inside one narrow region may provide less
meaningful coverage than a smaller, deliberately structured Scenario Set
spanning materially distinct regions.

Coverage Density

Coverage Density describes how intensively a region of Scenario Space has been
examined.

Possible conceptual states include:

Dense

Moderate

Sparse

Uncovered

or:

Unknown.

Density alone does not determine importance.

A sparse low-materiality region may be acceptable.

A sparse critical transition region may represent a major Coverage Gap.

Nominal Coverage

Nominal Coverage examines expected operating conditions.

It provides an important baseline.

However:

Nominal Coverage ≠ Scenario Coverage Sufficiency.

Variation Coverage

Variation Coverage asks whether materially relevant changes identified under
SCVM are sufficiently represented.

It may examine: range, direction, combinations, dependencies, resolution, and
configuration diversity.

Edge-Case Coverage

Edge-Case Coverage examines whether the materially relevant Edge Cases
identified under SECM have been: represented, configured, executed where
applicable, or explicitly recorded as gaps.

Stress-Condition Coverage

Stress-Condition Coverage examines whether the materially relevant Stress
Conditions established under SSCM have been sufficiently represented.

This may include: individual stresses, progressive stresses, sustained
stresses, compound stresses, cascading stresses, and recovery stresses.

Boundary Coverage

Boundary Coverage examines whether behavior near materially relevant limits
has been represented.

This may include:

Below Boundary

At Boundary

Above Boundary

where applicable.

Threshold Coverage

Threshold Coverage examines regions where system behavior, authority, control,
safety mode, or another state changes materially.

A simulation may require greater Scenario Resolution around thresholds than
within stable regions.

Transition Coverage

Transition Coverage examines movement between states.

Examples include: startup, shutdown, manual-to-autonomous transfer,
autonomous-to-human handover, primary-to-backup transition,
nominal-to-degraded mode, failure-to-recovery transition, mission-phase
change, or system reconfiguration.

SIMULOS® establishes:

Coverage of State A and State B does not establish coverage of the transition
between them.

Interaction Coverage

Interaction Coverage examines whether materially relevant combinations of
conditions have been represented.

This is especially important where system behavior is nonlinear.

Coverage of:

Condition A

and:

Condition B

does not establish coverage of:

A × B.

Higher-Order Interaction Coverage

Complex systems may exhibit behavior only when three or more conditions
interact.

However, exhaustive combinatorial simulation may become impossible.

SCCM therefore governs material interaction coverage, not blind enumeration of
every combination.

Combinatorial Explosion

As the number of Scenario Dimensions increases, the possible Scenario Space
can grow extremely rapidly.

For example, if ten independent dimensions each contain ten possible states,
the naive combination space becomes:

10¹⁰ possible configurations.

Running every configuration may be impractical or meaningless.

SCCM therefore requires deliberate governance over: materiality, interaction
relevance, coverage prioritization, scenario reduction, and residual
uncertainty.

No Exhaustive Enumeration Requirement

SIMULOS® establishes:

Scenario Coverage does not require exhaustive enumeration where exhaustive
enumeration is infeasible or does not materially improve the Simulation
Purpose.

The requirement is governed sufficiency, not brute-force completeness.

Temporal Coverage

Temporal Coverage examines whether materially relevant system behavior across
time is represented.

This may include: initialization, stable operation, transition, prolonged
operation, degradation, recovery, repeated cycles, or end-of-life behavior.

Sequence Coverage

Sequence Coverage examines whether materially different event orders have been
represented where ordering can affect system behavior.

Environmental Coverage

Environmental Coverage examines whether the range and interaction of
materially relevant environmental conditions are represented.

Configuration Coverage

Configuration Coverage examines whether materially distinct: hardware,
software, model, subsystem, parameter, mission, or operational configurations

are sufficiently represented.

Dependency Coverage

Dependency Coverage examines relevant states of external or internal
dependencies.

Examples include:

Available

Degraded

Intermittent

Unavailable

Recovering

where material.

Failure Coverage

Failure Coverage asks whether materially relevant failure states and pathways
have been represented.

This does not require deliberately causing every conceivable failure.

It requires visibility over the failure conditions relevant to the Simulation
Purpose.

Recovery Coverage

Recovery Coverage asks whether the Scenario Set sufficiently represents:
recovery initiation, partial recovery, fallback, restart, restoration,
degraded recovery, failed recovery, and repeated recovery

where material.

Human-System Interaction Coverage

Where humans materially interact with the Simulation Object, SCCM may examine
coverage of: normal intervention, delayed intervention, incorrect
intervention, conflicting intervention, absent intervention, authority
handover, misunderstanding, and workload conditions.

Multi-Agent Coverage

For multi-agent systems, Scenario Coverage may include: agent population, role
diversity, coordination structures, competing objectives, cooperation,
conflict, communication patterns, resource competition, and emergent group
behavior.

Autonomous-Behavior Coverage

Autonomous systems may require deliberate coverage of: authority states,
permission states, fallback behavior, escalation, override, autonomous
recovery, ambiguous information, changing objectives, coordination, and
boundary behavior.

Known Scenario Space

Known Scenario Space contains materially relevant conditions that have been
identified and can be represented sufficiently to participate in coverage
assessment.

Unknown Scenario Space

Complex systems inevitably contain conditions that have not yet been
identified.

SCCM therefore explicitly recognizes:

Unknown Scenario Space.

This is particularly important for: adaptive AI, autonomous systems,
multi-agent systems, novel operational environments, and complex mission
systems.

No Known-Space Completeness Assumption

SIMULOS® establishes:

Complete coverage of the currently known Scenario Space does not establish
that all materially relevant Scenario Space is known.

Coverage State Model

A Scenario Space region may be classified as:

Covered

The required Scenario Coverage has been sufficiently established.

Conditionally Covered

Coverage is sufficient only under defined conditions or assumptions.

Partially Covered

Some materially relevant portions remain insufficiently represented.

Sparse

Representation exists but is materially limited.

Uncovered

The relevant region has not been sufficiently represented.

Excluded

The region is intentionally outside the governed Scenario Set with a
documented basis.

Infeasible

The region is materially relevant but cannot currently be represented
sufficiently.

Unknown

The existence, extent, or relevance of the region remains insufficiently
known.

Scenario Coverage Gap

A Scenario Coverage Gap exists where a materially relevant portion of Scenario
Space is: absent, insufficiently represented, insufficiently varied,
insufficiently stressed, insufficiently resolved, poorly understood, or
currently infeasible to simulate.

Coverage Gap ≠ Simulation Failure

A Coverage Gap does not automatically mean the Simulation Object is unsafe,
invalid, or unsuitable.

It means:

the simulation basis does not yet provide sufficient information about that
region.

This distinction is essential.

Coverage Gap Materiality

Coverage Gaps may be assessed according to: operational relevance,
consequence, uncertainty, system sensitivity, probability where known,
boundary proximity, interaction potential, failure severity, recovery
significance, and downstream reliance.

Critical Coverage Gap

A Critical Coverage Gap may exist where a materially important region remains
insufficiently examined and the consequence of unknown behavior is high.

Examples may include: spacecraft autonomous recovery, collision avoidance,
medical-device failure mode, autonomous industrial shutdown, critical
infrastructure failover, or high-consequence AI authority transition.

Coverage Gap Register

SCCM may maintain a Scenario Coverage Gap Register containing: Coverage Gap
ID, affected Scenario Space, originating requirement, gap classification,
materiality, consequence, uncertainty, reason for gap, feasibility, planned
treatment, responsible governance process, status, and version.

Coverage Exclusion

A Scenario Space region may be intentionally excluded.

Possible bases include: outside Simulation Scope, physically impossible,
operationally irrelevant, materially immaterial, duplicate representation, or
another governed reason.

No Convenience Exclusion Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

A materially relevant Scenario Space must not be classified as excluded merely
because it is expensive, difficult, computationally demanding, data-poor, or
likely to produce unfavorable results.

Such conditions may create:

Coverage Limitations

or:

Coverage Gaps.

They do not erase relevance.

Infeasible Coverage

Some materially relevant Scenario Space may not be simulatable with current
capabilities.

SCCM preserves this as:

Material Scenario Space — Coverage
Infeasible

rather than silently removing it from the denominator of the governance
inquiry.

Coverage Confidence

Coverage Confidence expresses how strongly the available Scenario Set supports
the claim that a defined Scenario Space region has been sufficiently
represented.

Coverage Confidence may depend upon: Scenario quality, representation
fidelity, input quality, variation quality, edge-case discovery, stress
design, model capability, uncertainty, and execution completeness.

Coverage Confidence ≠ System Confidence

SIMULOS® establishes:

Confidence that Scenario Space has been covered is not equivalent to
confidence that the simulated system will perform successfully within that
space.

Coverage governs examination.

Performance is an outcome of that examination.

Coverage Sufficiency

Coverage Sufficiency asks:

Is the governed Scenario Set sufficiently representative of materially
relevant Scenario Space for the declared Simulation Purpose?

Possible outcomes may include:

Sufficient

Conditionally Sufficient

Partially Sufficient

Insufficient

or:

Undetermined.

Coverage Sufficiency Is Purpose-Bound

The same Scenario Set may be:

Sufficient

for:

early design exploration

but:

Insufficient

for:

high-consequence deployment reliance.

Therefore every sufficiency conclusion must remain tied to the Simulation
Purpose.

No Universal Sufficiency Transfer

SIMULOS® establishes:

Scenario Coverage judged sufficient for one Simulation Purpose must not
automatically be represented as sufficient for another purpose, consequence
level, operational context, or reliance decision.

Coverage Saturation

A simulation program may eventually reach a point where additional scenarios
produce little new information.

SCCM may identify Coverage Saturation where repeated scenario expansion no
longer materially improves understanding of a defined region. Coverage
Saturation ≠ Universal Completeness

Saturation within a known region does not establish: complete Scenario Space,
complete edge discovery, complete stress discovery, or absence of unknown
scenarios.

Marginal Coverage Value

Additional simulation should ideally improve the governed understanding of
Scenario Space.

SCCM may therefore assess:

Marginal Coverage Value

—the additional Scenario Coverage gained from additional simulation effort.

Coverage Efficiency

Coverage Efficiency concerns how effectively simulation resources expand
materially useful Scenario Coverage.

This can help distinguish:

more simulation

from:

more useful simulation.

No Run-Volume Optimization Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

The objective is not to maximize the number of simulated scenarios; it is to
maximize materially useful Scenario Coverage relative to the Simulation
Purpose and available resources.

Coverage Prioritization

Where resources are finite, SCCM may prioritize Scenario Space according to:
materiality, consequence, uncertainty, operational likelihood, novelty,
boundary sensitivity, interaction complexity, failure severity, recovery
importance, and existing coverage density.

A conceptual relationship may be:

Coverage Priority ∝ Materiality × Consequence × Uncertainty × Coverage Deficit

This is a governance relationship, not a mandatory mathematical formula.

Adaptive Coverage Expansion

Simulation results may reveal where additional coverage is needed.

The lifecycle can become:

Initial Scenario Set

↓

Simulation Execution

↓

Unexpected Behavior / Sensitivity

↓

New Scenario Requirement

↓

Coverage Gap

↓

Scenario Expansion

↓

New Execution

↓

Coverage Reassessment

This makes Scenario Coverage adaptive rather than static.

Coverage-Driven Simulation Planning

SCCM can support simulation planning by identifying where the next simulation
resources may create the greatest informational value.

Instead of:

run more simulations

the question becomes:

Which currently insufficiently covered region is most material to examine
next?

Coverage Reduction

Scenario Sets may also be reduced.

Redundant scenarios may be removed where: equivalent coverage remains,
material interactions are preserved, boundaries remain represented, stress
requirements remain represented, and traceability is maintained.

Coverage Compression

Large Scenario Sets may sometimes be represented through smaller strategically
selected sets.

Methods may include: equivalence classes, representative configurations,
boundary selection, combinatorial reduction, sensitivity-guided selection, or
other justified methods.

SCCM governs the coverage consequence.

It does not prescribe one mathematical technique.

No Untraceable Compression

Scenario reduction must not make it impossible to determine:

which material Scenario Space remains represented and which was lost.

Coverage Change

Scenario Coverage may change when: the Simulation Object changes, Simulation
Purpose changes, Simulation Scope changes, assumptions change, model
capability changes, inputs change, operating environment changes, new edge
cases are discovered, new stress conditions emerge, incidents occur, or new
operational evidence becomes available.

Coverage Versioning

Materially different coverage bases should remain distinguishable.

A Coverage Version may identify: Scenario Set version, object version, scope,
assumptions, model version, input basis, edge-case basis, stress basis, known
gaps, and date.

Historical Coverage Preservation

New Scenario Coverage must not rewrite previous simulation history.

SIMULOS® establishes:

A newly discovered scenario cannot be retroactively treated as having been
covered by earlier simulation campaigns that did not examine it.

Point-in-Time Coverage

SCCM supports questions such as:

What Scenario Space was considered known at the time?

Which scenarios had actually been represented?

Which Coverage Gaps were known?

Which exclusions were active?

Which critical conditions had not yet been discovered?

This can become important for: audits, incidents, validation, accountability,
certification, and operational decision reconstruction.

Coverage Heat Map

Complex simulation programs may represent Scenario Coverage through a Coverage
Heat Map.

This can reveal: dense nominal regions, sparse edge regions, uncovered
transitions, poorly represented interactions, and critical stress gaps.

For organizations operating thousands or millions of simulation runs, this may
provide more decision value than raw execution counts.

Coverage Bottleneck

A Coverage Bottleneck is the materially weakest region limiting the usefulness
of the overall Scenario Set for its intended purpose.

The bottleneck may be: one missing transition, one unknown dependency
interaction, one critical recovery state, one poorly represented environment,
or one unsimulated compound stress.

Therefore:

Overall Scenario Volume may be high while effective Scenario Coverage remains
bounded by a small number of critical gaps.

Scenario Coverage and Simulation Value

Scenario Coverage directly influences the informational value of simulation.

A highly accurate model executing poorly selected scenarios may produce
precise answers to the wrong questions.

SCCM therefore creates a governance bridge between:

simulation capability

and:

simulation usefulness.

Relationship to SCIM

SCIM establishes the Scenario population that materially belongs in the
inquiry.

SCCM determines whether that population is sufficiently represented.

Relationship to SCVM

SCVM defines materially relevant variation.

SCCM determines whether that Variation Space has been sufficiently covered.

Relationship to SECM

SECM identifies difficult edges.

SCCM determines whether those Edge Cases remain sufficiently represented or
constitute Coverage Gaps.

Relationship to SSCM

SSCM establishes materially relevant Stress Conditions.

SCCM determines whether those conditions are sufficiently represented within
the Scenario Set.

Relationship to Simulation Purpose & Scope

Coverage cannot be judged independently of purpose.

A Scenario Set is not simply:

well covered

or:

poorly covered.

It is:

sufficiently or insufficiently covered for a declared Simulation Purpose and
Scope.

Relationship to Reality Representation

Scenario Coverage cannot compensate for missing reality representation.

A simulation may have extensive Scenario Coverage over a Reality
Representation that omits a material part of the real system.

Therefore:

Scenario Coverage ≠ Reality Representation Completeness.

Relationship to Simulation Assumptions

Scenario Coverage may depend upon assumptions.

If an assumption excludes a class of conditions, the Coverage Assessment
should remain aware of that boundary.

Relationship to Model & Environment

Scenario Coverage may exceed the capability of the current model or
environment.

This should create a visible:

Coverage Capability Gap

rather than an implicit assumption of coverage.

Relationship to SIDS

Scenario Coverage depends upon available Simulation Inputs & Data.

Insufficient input representativeness may constrain practical coverage even
where Scenario Requirements are correctly identified.

Relationship to Simulation Execution

SCCM establishes the Scenario Coverage basis that execution will instantiate.

Execution may subsequently reveal: new gaps, unexpected sensitivity, unknown
edge cases, new stress conditions, or redundant scenarios.

These findings may return to SCCM for reassessment.

Relationship to VALIDOS®

SCCM does not determine whether Scenario Coverage is sufficient to validate
the Simulation Object or another system.

It establishes a governed Scenario Coverage record.

VALIDOS® determines what validation conclusion that record may legitimately
support.

Relationship to ORA™

ORA™ may provide Operational Reality information showing: actual operating
conditions, new environments, incidents, unexpected interactions, or emerging
conditions

that reveal Scenario Coverage Gaps.

SCCM governs their effect on Simulation Scenario Coverage.

Relationship to ASGA™

ASGA™ may identify autonomy-related operational spaces requiring Scenario
Coverage, including: authority transitions, permission boundaries, escalation,
override, coordination, fallback, and autonomous continuity.

SCCM governs whether these materially relevant spaces have been sufficiently
represented in simulation.

Relationship to AIG®

AIG® may identify AI behavioral conditions that require representation.

SCCM does not govern AI behavior itself.

It governs whether the relevant behavioral conditions are sufficiently
represented within the Scenario Set.

Space and Mission Systems

Scenario Coverage becomes especially important in space systems because:
real-world testing opportunities are limited, mission conditions may be
difficult to reproduce, many failures are unrecoverable after deployment,
environmental combinations are large, communication can be delayed, autonomy
may increase when ground control becomes unavailable, and rare transitions can
carry extreme consequence.

A space agency may run millions of Monte Carlo simulations.

SCCM asks a different question:

What materially relevant mission Scenario Space do those millions of runs
actually cover?

The simulation campaign may be dense around: nominal trajectory, ordinary
navigation uncertainty, standard communication conditions, and expected power
behavior

while remaining sparse around: autonomous recovery during communication loss,
compound power and navigation degradation, rare deployment transitions, sensor
disagreement during mission-phase change, or recovery after multiple
simultaneous subsystem degradations.

The bottleneck is therefore not necessarily:

insufficient simulation volume.

It may be:

insufficient governance over what the simulation volume actually represents.

Monte Carlo Coverage Governance

Monte Carlo simulation can generate enormous numbers of runs.

However, random or probabilistic sampling does not automatically guarantee
material Scenario Coverage.

SCCM may ask:

Which dimensions were sampled?

Which distributions governed sampling?

Which rare but material regions received little probability mass?

Which interactions were effectively never sampled?

Which boundaries were underrepresented?

Which operational states were excluded by the sampling model?

Which high-consequence low-frequency conditions require deliberate scenario
injection rather than waiting for random occurrence?

This creates an important distinction:

Statistical Sampling Coverage

versus:

Governed Material Scenario Coverage.

Autonomous Spacecraft Example

Scenario

An autonomous spacecraft navigation system undergoes:

8 million simulation runs.

Management initially interprets the volume as strong evidence of broad
coverage. Application

SCCM maps the runs against: mission phases, communication states, navigation
uncertainty, sensor disagreement, power states, autonomy levels, recovery
states, edge conditions, and compound stresses.

The mapping reveals:

7.4 million runs

are concentrated in nominal or near-nominal conditions.

Only a small fraction represent:

communication loss during autonomous recovery.

Almost none represent:

communication loss + low power + sensor disagreement during recovery
initiation. Result

The problem is not insufficient total simulation.

The problem is a Critical Scenario Coverage Gap hidden inside a very large
execution volume.

SCCM allows simulation resources to be redirected toward the materially weak
region.

Autonomous Vehicle Example

Scenario

An autonomous vehicle has extensive simulation coverage across weather, road
types, pedestrians, and traffic. Application

SCCM identifies strong individual-dimension coverage but weak interaction
coverage around:

construction zones + low visibility + temporary road markings + emergency
vehicle behavior. Result

The organization discovers that apparently broad scenario diversity contains a
material interaction gap.

Additional scenarios can be targeted precisely at the missing region.

AI Agent Example

Scenario

An AI agent has been simulated across
thousands of tasks. Application

SCCM maps: task type, permission state, tool availability, memory state,
information conflict, human oversight, execution latency, and authority
transitions.

Coverage is strong for task diversity.

It is weak for:

permission changes during active tool execution

and:

conflicting instructions during delayed external actions. Result

The organization can distinguish:

task coverage

from:

governance-critical behavioral coverage.

Industrial Robotics Example

Scenario

A manufacturer runs extensive robot simulations before deployment. Application

SCCM maps coverage across: payload, speed, human proximity, sensor confidence,
network availability, environmental conditions, production density, failure
states, and recovery behavior.

The organization discovers that:

high payload

and:

low sensor confidence

have both been extensively simulated individually.

Their interaction during:

human intervention

has almost no coverage. Result

A specific Coverage Gap becomes visible before operational deployment.

Diagnostic Application

SCCM can be applied to an existing simulation program without replacing its
simulation technology.

An organization can ask:

What Scenario Space are we claiming to cover?

Which parts are actually covered?

Which are merely sampled heavily?

Which dimensions have strong individual coverage but weak interaction
coverage?

Where are the edges?

Where are the stress conditions?

Which transitions are missing?

Which recovery states are missing?

Which critical gaps are hidden by high run volume?

Which regions are excluded?

Which regions cannot currently be simulated?

What remains unknown?

Which next simulation runs would produce the greatest material coverage
improvement?

This makes SCCM directly usable as a simulation-program diagnostic layer.

Module Boundary

SCCM begins when the governed outputs of:

Scenario Identification

Scenario Variation

Scenario Edge Cases

and:

Scenario Stress Conditions

are sufficiently available to define the materially relevant Scenario Space.

SCCM ends when: Coverage Requirements are established, Scenario Space is
mapped, material Coverage Dimensions are identified, covered and
insufficiently covered regions are distinguished, Coverage Gaps are
documented, exclusions and infeasible regions remain visible, Coverage
Sufficiency is assessed, and a Governed Scenario Coverage Basis is available
for Simulation Execution and downstream interpretation.

SCCM does not govern: Simulation Execution itself, simulation-output
interpretation, Simulation Validity, operational deployment authorization, or
final Validation Determination.

Minimum Implementation Framework

1. Define the Scenario Space

Integrate the governed outputs of: SCIM, SCVM, SECM, and SSCM.

2. Establish Coverage Dimensions

Identify the dimensions across which coverage materially matters. 3. Establish
Coverage Requirements

Determine the required breadth, depth, boundary representation, interaction
representation, stress representation, and other relevant coverage conditions.
4. Map Existing Scenario Coverage

Classify Scenario Space regions as: Covered, Conditionally Covered, Partially
Covered, Sparse, Uncovered, Excluded, Infeasible, or Unknown.

5. Identify Scenario Coverage Gaps

Record materially insufficient regions and their consequences. 6. Assess
Coverage Materiality

Architectural Position

Scenario Coverage Module (SCCM) is the fifth and final internal governance
space of the Simulation Scenario Standard (SSS).

The complete progression is:

Scenario Identification

↓

Scenario Variation

↓

Scenario Edge Cases

↓

Scenario Stress Conditions

↓

Scenario Coverage

Together:

SCIM establishes the governed Scenario population.

SCVM establishes materially relevant Scenario Variation.

SECM identifies difficult edges, thresholds, transitions, sparse regions, and
unusual interactions.

SSCM establishes deliberate Stress Conditions capable of exposing degradation,
limits, failure, cascading effects, and recovery.

SCCM determines whether the resulting Scenario Set sufficiently represents the
materially relevant Scenario Space and makes remaining gaps visible.

The output of the complete Parent Standard becomes:

Governed Scenario Space Governed Scenario
Set Scenario Coverage Basis

ready for downstream Simulation Execution.

Foundational Principle

Simulation confidence must not be inferred from how many scenarios were
executed, but from whether the materially relevant Scenario Space—including
variations, boundaries, interactions, edge cases, stress conditions,
transitions, failures, and recovery states—was sufficiently and transparently
represented.

Canonical Closing Statement

Scenario Coverage Module completes the internal governance lifecycle of the
Simulation Scenario Standard by establishing how materially relevant Scenario
Space is mapped against the scenarios actually selected for examination.
Through multidimensional Coverage Requirements, nominal and variation
coverage, edge and stress coverage, boundary and transition coverage,
interaction and temporal coverage, Coverage Gap identification, unknown-space
preservation, coverage sufficiency, prioritization, adaptive expansion,
compression governance, point-in-time traceability, and coverage-efficiency
analysis, SCCM prevents simulation volume from being mistaken for simulation
breadth and transforms Scenario Coverage into an explicit governable object.
It enables organizations to determine not merely how many simulations they
have run, but which parts of the problem space those simulations actually
illuminate, which critical regions remain weak or unknown, and where the next
unit of simulation effort can produce the greatest material improvement in
understanding.
