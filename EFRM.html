Canonical Definition

Event Formation Root Module (EFRM) governs how the operational
root association of a developing Event Formation is identified,
differentiated, qualified, related, and preserved so that the
formation can be anchored to the materially relevant operational
basis from which its structure originates or becomes meaningfully
organized.

EFRM establishes the governed relationship between a formed event
structure and the operational occurrence, condition, transition,
action, interaction, or state change that provides the most
relevant structural anchor for understanding how that formation
came into being.

Its task is specific:

to establish the governed operational root association of Event
Formation without converting that root into identity origin,
provenance, legal causality, legal tax point, Event Boundary, or
jurisdiction-specific tax determination.

Operational Role

EFCM establishes:

which elements qualify as Event Formation Components.

EFAM establishes:

how those components are assembled into a coherent formation
structure.

EFDM establishes:

what that formation materially depends upon.

EFRM then determines:

what operational basis most appropriately anchors the formation
as its Event Formation Root.

This distinction is necessary because a formation may contain
multiple components and depend on multiple prior conditions while
still requiring a coherent operational anchor.

For example:

a payment formation may be rooted in execution of a transfer
instruction.

A service formation may be rooted in commencement or activation
of the governed service interaction.

A production formation may be rooted in a materially relevant
production initiation.

An autonomous transaction may be rooted in an autonomous
execution action.

A machine-to-machine formation may be rooted in a governed
machine interaction or operational state transition.

The Event Formation Root provides structural orientation.

It does not determine legal origin, ownership, identity
provenance, responsibility, or taxation.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Identity Origin.

Event Formation Root ≠ Legal Tax Point.

Operational Space

EFRM governs the space between:

an assembled Event Formation with governed material dependencies

and

an Event Formation whose operational root association is
sufficiently established for Formation Integrity governance.

Its operational space includes: root candidate identification;
operational root relevance; root differentiation; root
qualification; root exclusion; direct root association; indirect
root association; composite root association; distributed root
association; machine-generated roots; autonomous roots; root
ambiguity; root conflict; root uncertainty; root traceability;
root reconsideration.

EFRM governs the operational anchor of Event Formation.

It does not determine who originally created an identity, who
owns an asset, who possesses authority, who is responsible, where
legal causation begins, when tax legally arises, or what belongs
inside the final Event Boundary.

Operational Scope

EFRM may govern Event Formation Roots associated with: commercial
transactions; payments; financial operations; transfers;
production; supply chains; fulfilment; logistics; service
delivery; digital services; subscriptions; platforms;
marketplaces; banking systems; enterprise systems; APIs; software
interactions; digital assets; infrastructure operations; AI
agents; autonomous systems; robots; machine-to-machine
transactions; automated marketplaces; distributed systems; hybrid
physical-digital environments; future machine economies.

An Event Formation Root may arise from human, organizational,
transactional, digital, operational, machine-generated, or
autonomous activity.

EFRM remains actor-neutral and technology-neutral.

Event Formation Root Principle

Complex formations frequently contain many plausible starting
points.

The first technical record is not necessarily the operational
root.

The earliest timestamp is not necessarily the operational root.

The first participant action is not necessarily the operational
root.

The oldest dependency is not necessarily the operational root.

The Event Formation Root must instead represent the operational
basis that materially anchors the formation being governed.

Therefore:

Earliest Record ≠ Event Formation Root by Default.

First Technical Action ≠ Event Formation Root by Default.

Oldest Dependency ≠ Event Formation Root by Default.

Root Candidate

A Root Candidate is an operational occurrence, action,
interaction, transition, or state that may provide the materially
relevant structural anchor for Event Formation.

Possible Root Candidates may include: transaction initiation;
execution instruction; service activation; resource commitment;
production initiation; asset transfer initiation; machine
execution; autonomous action; platform interaction; state
transition; governed system trigger; coordinated multi-system
initiation.

Candidate status does not establish the root.

Therefore:

Root Candidate ≠ Qualified Event Formation Root.

Operational Root Relevance

A Root Candidate should be qualified only where it materially
explains the structural emergence or organization of the
formation.

Relevant considerations may include whether the candidate:
materially initiates the formation; creates the operational
condition around which components assemble; establishes the
principal structural transition represented by the formation;
provides a coherent anchor for associated components;
distinguishes the formation from surrounding activity; remains
consistent with governed dependencies; can be sufficiently traced
to the recognized occurrence.

Root qualification must not be based solely on technical
convenience. Technical Origin ≠ Operational Root

Digital and enterprise systems often create several technical
origins for the same activity.

A transaction may produce: API requests; database entries;
message-queue events; accounting records; audit logs; settlement
instructions; system notifications.

The earliest technical artifact does not automatically define the
Event Formation Root.

Therefore:

Technical Origin ≠ Operational Root.

EFRM must preserve the distinction between the system artifact
that first records activity and the operational basis that
materially anchors the event formation. Dependency ≠ Root

EFDM may establish several material dependencies supporting the
formation.

EFRM must not automatically select one of those dependencies as
the Event Formation Root.

For example:

an autonomous purchase may depend on prior delegation.

The delegation may be essential.

But the purchase formation may be operationally rooted in the
autonomous execution of the purchase rather than the earlier
delegation.

Therefore:

Formation Dependency ≠ Event Formation
Root by Default. Trigger ≠ Root

A technical or operational trigger may initiate a process without
constituting the Event Formation Root.

For example:

a scheduled timer may trigger an autonomous system.

A sensor may trigger a machine workflow.

A notification may trigger an API call.

A price threshold may trigger an algorithm.

The trigger may explain why execution began.

The root must explain what operational basis anchors the
formation itself.

Therefore:

Trigger ≠ Event Formation Root by Default.
Event Root ≠ Identity Origin

EFRM does not determine where an actor, organization, machine,
account, asset, agent, or digital identity originated.

Identity origin belongs to the architecture governing identity
and origin.

Within the OOF architecture ecosystem, this space is governed
principally through OBIDENITY® where applicable.

EFRM may consume identity references where necessary.

It does not redefine them.

Therefore:

Event Root ≠ Identity Origin.

Root Association ≠ Provenance Governance.
Event Root ≠ Ownership Origin

The operational root of an event does not determine ownership.

A transfer formation may be rooted in execution of a transfer.

That does not determine who legally owned the transferred object
before or after that execution.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Ownership Determination. Event Root ≠
Authority Origin

An event may be executed under delegated or inherited authority.

The existence of that authority may be a dependency.

The operational root may nevertheless be the execution itself.

EFRM does not determine where the authority originated or whether
it was legally valid.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Authority Origin.
Event Root ≠ Legal Causality

The operational root provides a structural anchor.

It does not establish legal causation.

The operational point from which an event formation is organized
may differ from the point considered legally causal under
contract, tort, regulatory, tax, or other law.

Therefore:

Operational Root ≠ Legal Cause. Event
Formation Root ≠ Legal Tax Point

A particularly important TREGA™ boundary concerns taxation.

EFRM may determine that a particular operational action or
transition provides the most appropriate root for Event
Formation.

It must not determine that tax legally arises at that point.

Tax legislation may establish a legally relevant tax point using
rules concerning: supply; delivery; invoicing; payment; transfer;
realization; recognition; residence; permanent establishment;
jurisdiction-specific timing rules.

Those determinations remain outside EFRM.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Legal Tax Point.
Root ≠ Event Start Time

The Event Formation Root may be associated with a particular
operational moment.

That does not make EFRM the owner of Event Timing.

TRETS later governs Event Timing.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Event Start Time Determination.

EFRM may preserve relevant temporal references without
determining the governed Event Start, Completion, Duration, or
Temporal Order. Root ≠ Event Location

An operational root may occur through a particular system,
facility, machine, platform, or geographic environment.

EFRM does not determine governed Event Location.

TRELS owns that space.

Therefore:

Root Association ≠ Event Location Determination.

However, a single root should not be imposed merely for

Composite Root Formation

Some event formations may emerge from several operational actions
that jointly provide the structural basis of the formation.

EFRM must therefore permit Composite Root Association where no
single operational element legitimately represents the formation.

Therefore:

Multiple Root Elements ≠ Multiple Events by Default.

A composite root does not itself determine whether one or
multiple final events exist.

Distributed Root Formation

In distributed systems, no single technical system may contain
the complete operational root.

A cross-platform transaction may emerge through coordinated
actions across: marketplace; payment network; service provider;
autonomous agent; infrastructure; distributed ledger; external
API.

EFRM must permit a root to be represented across multiple
operational sources where necessary.

Therefore:

Distributed Root Representation ≠ Multiple Event Roots by
Default.

Machine-Generated Root

A machine-generated formation may possess a legitimate
operational root even where no human initiates the immediate
action.

Possible examples include: automated resource purchase;
machine-triggered replenishment; algorithmic settlement; robotic
service execution; autonomous infrastructure allocation.

Therefore:

Human Initiation Is Not Required for Event Formation Root.

However:

Machine Execution ≠ Independent Event Root by Default.

Material operational relevance remains required.

Autonomous Event Formation Root

Autonomous agents may create formations through independently
executed operational decisions.

EFRM may establish an autonomous execution as the Event Formation
Root where it materially anchors the formation.

This does not establish: autonomous legal personality; taxpayer
status; legal responsibility; ownership; authority; tax
liability.

Therefore:

Autonomous Event Root ≠ Autonomous Legal Responsibility.

Different systems or evidence sources may identify different
plausible operational roots.

For example:

a marketplace may identify order acceptance as the root.

A payment system may identify payment authorization.

A supplier system may identify service activation.

EFRM must not silently collapse materially different
representations.

Root Reconsideration

New information may materially alter the basis on which the Event
Formation Root was established.

EFRM must permit governed reconsideration.

Reconsideration must preserve: previous root association;
previous basis; reason for reconsideration; new information;
revised root association; temporal sequence of changes.

Therefore:

Root Reconsideration ≠ Historical Erasure.

Root Traceability

A governed Event Formation Root should remain traceable to its
basis.

Where materially required, traceability may preserve: root
identifier; root candidate references; qualification basis;
associated formation; relevant dependencies; source references;
uncertainty; conflict; composite-root structure; revision
history; reconsideration history.

Traceability enables subsequent governance to understand why a
particular operational root was associated with the formation.

Root Independence from Event Boundary

EFRM operates before TREBS determines final Event Boundary.

The operational root may help subsequent Boundary governance
understand the formation.

It does not establish which elements ultimately belong inside or
outside the event.

Therefore:

Event Formation Root ≠ Event Boundary.

A root may anchor an event without defining its complete extent.

Root Independence from Event Determination

EFRM does not determine that a Tax-Relevant Event formally
exists.

TREDS later owns Governed Event Determination.

EFRM establishes only one structural dimension required for
formation.

Therefore:

Root Established ≠ Event Determined.

Root Independence from Tax Rules

Event Formation Root should remain governed independently of
jurisdiction-specific tax interpretation.

A subsequent change in tax law should not automatically require
the operational root of the historical event to be rewritten.

Therefore:

Tax Rule Change ≠ Event Root Change by Default.

The same governed Event Formation Root may support multiple
downstream jurisdictional evaluations.

Governance Requirements

EFRM requires that: Root Candidates are evaluated against the
governed Event Formation; root qualification is based on material
operational relevance; the earliest record is not automatically
treated as the root; technical origin is distinguished from
operational root; dependencies are not automatically converted
into roots; triggers are not automatically converted into roots;
Event Root remains distinct from Identity Origin; Root
Association remains distinct from provenance; Event Root remains
distinct from ownership and authority origin; Event Root remains
distinct from legal causality; Event Root remains distinct from
legal tax point; Event Root does not determine Event Timing;
Event Root does not determine Event Location; single, composite,
and distributed roots may be represented where appropriate;
machine and autonomous roots may be governed without requiring
human initiation; root conflict remains visible; unresolved roots
remain explicitly unresolved; root reconsideration preserves
history; root decisions remain traceable; root establishment does
not determine Event Boundary; root establishment does not
determine legal taxability.

Governance Logic

EFRM applies focused root-association logic:

What operational occurrence, action, interaction, transition, or
state most materially anchors this Event Formation?

Is the candidate an operational root or merely a dependency,
trigger, technical record, or surrounding condition?

Can one root be legitimately established, or is a composite or
distributed root required?

Are competing root representations materially conflicting?

Is the root sufficiently established to proceed into Formation
Integrity governance?

Enterprise Application

EFRM addresses a recurring problem in enterprise, platform,
financial, autonomous, and distributed systems:

different systems often define the “origin” of the same event
according to whichever technical record they control.

A marketplace may use order creation.

A payment system may use authorization.

An ERP may use invoice creation.

A logistics system may use dispatch.

A cloud platform may use service activation.

An autonomous system may use machine execution.

A tax engine may later use a legally defined tax point.

These are not automatically the same thing.

EFRM establishes a separate governed concept:

the Event Formation Root.

This creates an operational anchor that can remain stable even
when different systems, jurisdictions, accounting methods, or tax
rules subsequently interpret the event differently. Use CasesUse
Case 1 — Cross-Border Digital Transaction Has Five Competing
“Origins”

A global digital platform provides a service involving: customer
order creation in Country A; payment authorization through
infrastructure in Country B; automated service activation in
Country C; cloud execution across several regions; invoicing from
an entity in Country D.

Each system records a different apparent starting point.

A tax engine may additionally apply jurisdiction-specific rules
to determine a legally relevant tax point.

Without EFRM, the operational event may be reconstructed
differently depending on which system or jurisdiction examines
it.

EFRM evaluates the formation independently of those downstream
legal rules.

It may determine that service activation provides the materially
relevant operational root for the governed Event Formation while
preserving order creation and payment authorization as separate
components or dependencies where appropriate.

That determination does not establish: tax jurisdiction; tax
point; taxpayer; tax liability.

The result is one stable operational Event Formation Root that
multiple jurisdictional systems may subsequently evaluate under
their own laws.

Canonical Closing Statement

Event Formation Root Module establishes the operational anchor of
Tax-Relevant Event Formation by determining what occurrence,
action, interaction, transition, or state materially roots a
governed formation without confusing that root with technical
origin, dependency, trigger, Identity Origin, provenance,
ownership, authority, legal causality, Event Timing, Event
Location, Event Boundary, or legal tax point. By permitting
single, composite, distributed, machine-generated, and autonomous
root associations while preserving conflict, uncertainty,
traceability, and reconsideration, EFRM creates a stable
operational reference for complex event formations before
Formation Integrity is assessed. The Module therefore anchors
what the event formation operationally grows from while
preserving the foundational TREGA™ boundary: the operational root
explains the formation — it does not determine the tax.
