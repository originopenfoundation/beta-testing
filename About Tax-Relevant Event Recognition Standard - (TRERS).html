About Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard -
(TRERS)

Should Enter Tax-Relevant Event Governance

Modern economic systems continuously generate activity.

Payments are authorized.

Orders are created.

APIs are called.

Assets move.

Machines execute instructions.

Platforms create records.

Autonomous agents purchase services.

Distributed systems produce thousands or millions of technical
signals around economic activity.

But the existence of a signal or system record does not establish
that a distinct occurrence should enter tax-relevant event
governance.

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard (TRERS) establishes the
governed recognition layer required to distinguish potentially
relevant occurrences from technical noise, duplicate records,
background operations, and activity that does not legitimately
require progression through the TREGA™ lifecycle.

Its purpose is to answer a fundamental governance question:

Which contextualized occurrences should legitimately proceed
toward formation as potential Tax-Relevant Events? What Is
Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard?

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard (TRERS) is the second
Parent Standard of TREGA™ — Tax-Relevant Event Governance
Architecture™.

It establishes the governance conditions required to evaluate a
sufficiently contextualized real-world, economic, operational,
transactional, digital, autonomous, machine-generated, or
value-affecting occurrence and determine whether it should be
recognized as a legitimate candidate for further tax-relevant
event governance.

TRERS operates after TRECS — Tax-Relevant Event Context Standard
has established the contextual environment necessary to
understand the occurrence.

It operates before TREFS — Tax-Relevant Event Formation Standard
determines how a recognized occurrence becomes structurally
formed into a governed event.

Its role is therefore deliberately bounded:

TRERS determines whether an occurrence should enter Event
Formation. It does not determine whether tax is legally due.

Canonical Definition

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard (TRERS) defines the
governance conditions required to distinguish, screen, evaluate,
recognize, reject, escalate, and reconsider contextualized
occurrences according to their potential relevance to subsequent
tax-relevant event governance while preserving the boundaries
between technical detection, occurrence recognition, Event
Formation, Event Determination, and jurisdiction-specific legal
taxability.

Why TRERS Exists

Digital and automated economies increasingly create a structural
problem for taxation infrastructure:

systems can generate far more observable records than
economically meaningful events.

One commercial activity may produce:

an order record

An autonomous system may produce thousands of machine operations
around a single economic objective.

A distributed platform may represent the same underlying activity
independently across several systems.

Without governed recognition, downstream systems may incorrectly:
treat technical signals as independent economic occurrences;
create multiple event candidates from duplicate representations
of the same activity; ignore legitimate occurrences because no
single system contains the complete record; treat every
autonomous action as independently tax-relevant; confuse system
activity with economic activity; collapse genuinely distinct
occurrences because they share an account or identifier; convert
potential tax relevance directly into assumed legal taxability;
apply inconsistent recognition logic across systems, platforms,
or jurisdictions.

TRERS exists to establish a controlled gateway between observable
activity and formal Event Formation.

The objective is neither to recognize everything nor to recognize
as little as possible.

The objective is to recognize what legitimately requires further
governance.

What TRERS Governs

TRERS governs Tax-Relevant Event Recognition, including:
occurrence recognition; occurrence differentiation;
signal-to-occurrence qualification; potential tax-event relevance
screening; recognition criteria; recognition rules; recognition
confidence; recognition uncertainty; ambiguous occurrence
treatment; duplicate recognition risk; machine-generated
occurrence recognition; autonomous occurrence recognition;
distributed occurrence recognition; recognition rejection;
recognition escalation; recognition reconsideration; recognition
traceability.

The Standard allows recognition mechanisms to operate across
human, enterprise, digital, machine, and autonomous environments
without assuming that every observable action has the same
governance significance.

What TRERS Does Not Determine

TRERS deliberately does not determine: the complete structure of
the eventual Tax-Relevant Event; the final Event Boundary; Event
Timing; Event Location; Event Participation; taxpayer status;
Event-to-Value Association; relationships among formed events;
legal taxability; tax jurisdiction; tax nexus; taxable value; tax
base; applicable tax rate; exemption or deduction eligibility;
tax liability; reporting obligations; payment obligations;
enforcement.

Recognition means only that an occurrence has satisfied the
governance conditions required to proceed further through TREGA™.

Therefore:

Event Recognition ≠ Taxability Determination.

And more fundamentally:

Tax-Relevant Event ≠ Legally Taxable Event.

Why the Distinction Matters

Conventional tax infrastructure often begins from records
produced by operational systems.

But operational systems are designed for their own purposes.

A payment system records payments.

A logistics system records movements.

A marketplace records platform interactions.

A robot records machine actions.

An ERP records enterprise transactions.

An API records technical requests.

None of those systems necessarily defines an economically
meaningful Tax-Relevant Event.

If downstream taxation infrastructure simply treats each
technical record as an event, the representation of economic
reality may become fragmented, duplicated, or distorted.

TRERS introduces a governance layer between technical
observability and event formation.

This makes it possible to distinguish:

what a system recorded

from

what legitimately warrants further event governance. Signal ≠
Occurrence

A signal indicates that something was detected or recorded.

It does not necessarily establish a distinct occurrence.

For example:

an API retry may create another log entry without another
economic action;

a payment confirmation may represent the same underlying activity
already represented by an authorization;

a sensor may report the same machine state repeatedly;

an ERP synchronization may reproduce an existing transaction in
another system.

TRERS therefore prevents raw observability from becoming
automatic event recognition.

Signal ≠ Occurrence. Occurrence ≠
Tax-Relevant Event

Even after an occurrence has been sufficiently distinguished from
technical signals, recognition remains only an intermediate
governance stage.

TRERS establishes that the occurrence warrants further
processing.

TREFS — Tax-Relevant Event Formation Standard must still
determine how the recognized occurrence is structurally formed.

Subsequent TREGA™ standards must still govern its boundary,
timing, location, participation, value association,
relationships, determination, record, and lifecycle.

Therefore:

Recognized Occurrence ≠ Formed Event.

And:

Recognized Occurrence ≠ Legally Taxable Event.

Recognition Without Premature Tax
Determination

An occurrence may have plausible relevance to taxation analysis
while its eventual legal treatment remains unknown.

TRERS deliberately permits such an occurrence to proceed.

This is necessary because legal tax consequences may depend on
downstream facts, jurisdiction-specific rules, exemptions,
thresholds, classifications, or other legal conditions that
TREGA™ does not own.

Recognition therefore answers:

Should this occurrence continue through event governance?

It does not answer:

Is tax legally due?

This preserves the foundational TREGA™ principle:

TREGA™ governs the event. The jurisdiction governs the tax.

Recognition Under Uncertainty

Real-world systems are not always complete.

Records may conflict.

Context may be incomplete.

A distributed transaction may be visible only partially.

A machine-generated action may have uncertain economic
significance.

TRERS does not require uncertainty to be converted artificially
into certainty.

An occurrence may remain unresolved or be escalated for
additional governance.

This protects the architecture from two opposite failures:

false recognition — activity is advanced without sufficient
basis;

and

false rejection — potentially relevant activity disappears
because certainty was unavailable immediately.

Machine and Autonomous Economy Relevance

Recognition becomes increasingly important as autonomous systems
participate directly in economic activity.

An AI agent may: discover a supplier; negotiate within delegated
parameters; invoke an API; reserve computing capacity; purchase a
service; allocate resources; initiate payment; coordinate another
autonomous system.

Hundreds or thousands of technical operations may support one
economically meaningful objective.

TRERS provides the recognition layer needed to prevent every
machine instruction from automatically becoming a Tax-Relevant
Event candidate.

At the same time, it prevents legitimate machine-generated
economic occurrences from being ignored merely because no human
manually initiated them.

Therefore:

Machine Activity ≠ Tax-Relevant Occurrence by Default.

Distributed Recognition

Modern economic activity may be distributed across systems that
each observe only part of what occurred.

For example:

↓

No individual system may contain enough information to represent

TRERS allows governed recognition to use distributed contextual
and evidential information where necessary.

The existence of several records does not automatically establish
several occurrences.

Therefore:

Multiple Records ≠ Multiple Occurrences.

Likewise, the use of a shared account, identifier, platform, or
infrastructure does not automatically establish that several
records represent one occurrence.

Recognition must remain governed.

Recognition Before Formation

Within TREGA™, recognition occupies a precise architectural
position:

Event Context

↓

Event Recognition

↓

Event Formation

TRECS asks:

What surrounding environment is necessary to understand the
occurrence?

TRERS asks:

Does this contextualized occurrence legitimately warrant further
tax-relevant event governance?

TREFS then asks:

How must the recognized occurrence be formed into a governed
event structure?

These stages must remain separated.

Context-Ready ≠ Event-Recognized.

Event-Recognized ≠ Event-Formed.

Event-Formed ≠ Legally Taxable.

TRERS establishes the recognition gateway between governed
contextual reality and formal Event Formation.

Its output is not a tax assessment.

Its output is a recognized occurrence sufficiently governed to
proceed toward Event Formation. Use Case 1 — Millions of Machine
Records Around One Economic Activity

An automated industrial environment contains robots, sensors,
production software, inventory systems, autonomous transport, ERP
infrastructure, and payment systems.

A single production and delivery process may generate tens of
thousands of technical records.

If each record is treated as an independent event candidate,
downstream taxation infrastructure can become overwhelmed with
artificial events that represent technical execution rather than
distinct economic activity.

TRERS screens contextualized occurrences and determines which
ones legitimately warrant progression into Event Formation.

Machine telemetry, retries, synchronization messages, and other
operational signals can remain outside further event governance
where they do not satisfy recognition conditions.

At the same time, economically meaningful machine-generated
activity can proceed even where no human manually initiated it.

TRERS therefore addresses a growing structural problem:

the number of machine-generated records can increase almost
without limit while the number of economically meaningful events
does not. Use Case 2 — One Cross-Border Activity Visible as
Different Events in Different Systems

A digital marketplace facilitates a cross-border service
transaction involving a customer, platform operator, service
provider, payment infrastructure, and automated settlement
system.

The marketplace records an order.

The payment provider records an authorization and settlement.

The service system records activation.

The accounting system creates another transaction.

The provider records delivery.

Without governed recognition, different systems may independently
present these records as separate event candidates, while another
system may collapse them into one record based only on a shared
identifier.

TRERS establishes whether the contextualized observations
represent occurrences that legitimately require separate
progression through Event Formation.

It does not decide how the final Tax-Relevant Event or events
will be structured, which jurisdiction may tax them, or whether
any tax is legally due.

This allows one underlying economic reality to be recognized
methodologically before different jurisdictional tax systems
subsequently interpret its legal consequences.

Canonical Closing Statement

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard establishes the
recognition governance layer of TREGA™ — Tax-Relevant Event
Governance Architecture™ by determining which sufficiently
contextualized real-world, economic, operational, transactional,
digital, autonomous, machine-generated, and value-affecting
occurrences legitimately warrant progression into formal Event
Formation. By separating technical signals from occurrences,
duplicate records from independent activity, machine execution
from economic relevance, recognition uncertainty from assumed
certainty, and potential tax relevance from legal taxability,
TRERS prevents operational systems from defining Tax-Relevant
Events merely through the records they happen to generate. The
Standard creates a governed recognition gateway through which
human, enterprise, digital, distributed, and autonomous economic
activity can enter the TREGA™ lifecycle while preserving the
boundaries between Event Context, Event Recognition, Event
Formation, Event Determination, and jurisdiction-specific
taxation. TRERS therefore establishes not whether tax is due, but
whether an occurrence has acquired sufficient governed relevance
to justify asking the next architectural question: what
Tax-Relevant Event, if any, should now be formed from it?
