Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Responsibility Traceability Standard (RTS) defines the structural
conditions under which responsibility relationships, responsibility
assignments, responsibility transfers, responsibility decisions,
responsibility actions, responsibility transitions, responsibility
outcomes, and responsibility-governance activities remain traceable,
reconstructable, reviewable, auditable, governable, enforceable, and
operationally valid throughout the responsibility lifecycle.

RTS governs responsibility traceability.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine how responsibility moved throughout operational reality,
who carried responsibility at specific points in time, how
responsibility changed, and whether the complete responsibility
chain can be reconstructed.

Responsibility may exist.

Responsibility may be assigned.

Responsibility may remain continuous.

RTS governs whether responsibility can be reconstructed.

A. Standard Abstract

Many governance systems know that responsibility existed.

Many governance systems cannot reconstruct responsibility afterward.

Investigations often fail because responsibility history is lost.

Governance often fails because responsibility pathways cannot
be reconstructed.

Without traceability governance:

  • responsibility chains become invisible
  • investigations become difficult
  • accountability weakens
  • evidence loses context
  • governance transparency deteriorates


RTS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Responsibility remains governance-valid only when responsibility
history remains traceable, reconstructable, reviewable, and
auditable throughout the responsibility lifecycle.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Many governance failures occur because responsibility cannot
be traced.

Examples include:

  • undocumented responsibility transfers
  • missing responsibility records
  • fragmented responsibility chains
  • contractor traceability failures
  • responsibility ambiguity
  • governance handover failures
  • Human-AI traceability failures
  • accountability reconstruction failures


The challenge is not identifying responsibility.

The challenge is reconstructing responsibility.

RTS exists because responsibility traceability is a
distinct governance space.

E. Responsibility Traceability Integrity Logic

Responsibility traceability integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Traceability Origin Integrity

Responsibility history remains connected to
legitimate responsibility sources.

2. Traceability Scope Integrity

Traceable responsibility relationships remain connected to clearly
defined governance boundaries.

3. Traceability Validity Integrity

Responsibility traceability remains legitimate and
operationally valid.

4. Traceability Reconstruction Integrity

Responsibility history remains reconstructable throughout
the responsibility lifecycle.

5. Traceability Accountability Integrity

Responsibility traceability remains connected to
accountable governance structures.

G. Difference Between Continuity and

Traceability

Responsibility Continuity asks:

Did responsibility remain preserved?

Responsibility Traceability asks:

Can responsibility be reconstructed?

Responsibility may remain continuous.

Yet continuity may not be reconstructable.

RCS governs preservation.

RTS governs reconstruction.

Both are required.

I. Responsibility Traceability Failure Rule

Responsibility traceability failure occurs when responsibility
history cannot be reliably reconstructed, reviewed, audited,
governed, validated, or connected to accountable
governance structures.

Examples include:

  • missing records
  • undocumented transitions
  • invisible responsibility chains
  • contractor traceability failures
  • governance transparency failures
  • Human-AI traceability breakdowns
  • responsibility reconstruction failures


RTS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A responsibility-traceability environment is valid under RTS when:

  • responsibility history remains visible
  • responsibility transitions remain reconstructable
  • responsibility-chain relationships remain reviewable
  • responsibility records remain auditable

  • governance transparency collapses
  • accountability reconstruction becomes impossible
