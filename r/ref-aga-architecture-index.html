Accountability Governance Architecture

Official Accountability Governance Map for Organizations,
Institutions, Governments, Autonomous Systems and Human-AI
Operational Environments AGA™ (Accountability Governance
Architecture) serves as the official OOF® accountability governance
map used for accountability space identification, accountability
governance classification, accountability diagnostics,
accountability validation, accountability architecture analysis, and
accountability problem placement across organizational,
institutional, technological, autonomous, and Human-AI environments.
AGA™ provides the primary accountability governance reference
architecture used to identify where accountability-related problems
belong before accountability analysis, accountability investigation,
accountability validation, accountability governance, or
accountability improvement begins. The purpose of AGA™ is not to
investigate accountability. The purpose of AGA™ is to identify and
define the governable accountability spaces of operational reality.

Core Architectural Principle

Every accountability space answers one primary question. No
accountability space duplicates the purpose of another
accountability space. Every accountability space begins where the
previous accountability space ends. Together these accountability
spaces form the official accountability governance map of AGA™.

1. Relationship Accountability Standard (RAS)

Core Question

What is the actual relationship between the actors?

Canonical Definition

RAS defines the conditions under which operational relationships
remain identifiable, reconstructable, governable, accountable, and
operationally valid.

Governance Boundary

Begins when two or more actors enter an operational relationship.
Ends when authority relationships can be evaluated.

2. Authority Governance Standard (AGS)

Core Question

Who has the authority to act?

Canonical Definition

AGS defines the conditions under which authority remains
identifiable, legitimate, governable, accountable, and
operationally valid.

Governance Boundary

Begins when authority exists. Ends when authority validity can
be evaluated.

3. Authority Validation Standard (AVS)

Core Question

Is the authority valid?

Canonical Definition

AVS defines the conditions under which authority remains legitimate,
recognized, enforceable, and governance-valid.

Governance Boundary

Begins when authority requires validation. Ends when authority
validity has been determined.

4. Authority Delegation Standard (ADS)

Core Question

Has authority been delegated?

Canonical Definition

ADS defines the conditions under which authority may be transferred,
delegated, accepted, preserved, and governed.

Governance Boundary

Begins when authority moves between actors. Ends when delegated
authority becomes operational.

5. Authority Escalation Standard (AES)

Core Question

Should authority have been escalated?

Canonical Definition

AES defines the conditions under which authority escalation becomes
required, justified, governable, and operationally valid.

Governance Boundary

Begins when authority exceeds operational limits. Ends when
escalation obligations can be evaluated.

6. Responsibility Governance Standard (RGS)

Core Question

Who had the obligation to act?

Canonical Definition

RGS defines the conditions under which responsibility ownership,
responsibility obligations, and responsibility governance remain
identifiable and enforceable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when responsibility exists. Ends when responsibility
assignment can be evaluated.

7. Responsibility Assignment Standard (RAS-A)

Core Question

To whom was responsibility assigned?

Canonical Definition

RAS-A defines the conditions under which responsibility assignment,
acceptance, ownership, and transfer remain governable
and accountable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when responsibility is assigned. Ends when responsibility
ownership can be evaluated.

8. Responsibility Continuity Standard (RCS)

Core Question

Did responsibility remain preserved?

Canonical Definition

RCS defines the conditions under which responsibility remains
preserved throughout operational and governance transitions.

Governance Boundary

Begins when responsibility changes over time. Ends when
responsibility continuity can be evaluated.

9. Responsibility Traceability Standard (RTS)

Core Question

Can responsibility be reconstructed?

Canonical Definition

RTS defines the conditions under which responsibility history
remains traceable, reconstructable, reviewable, and governable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when responsibility history exists. Ends when responsibility
reconstruction becomes possible.

10. Capability Readiness Governance Standard (CRGS)

Core Question

Was the actor capable of acting?

Canonical Definition

CRGS defines the conditions under which capability, competence,
qualifications, resources, readiness, and operational preparedness
remain governable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when an actor is expected to perform. Ends when capability
readiness can be evaluated.

11. Chain of Control Standard (CCS)

Core Question

Who actually controlled the outcome?

Canonical Definition

CCS defines the conditions under which operational control remains
identifiable, reconstructable, governable, and accountable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when operational influence exists. Ends when actual control
can be determined.

12. Governance Oversight Standard (GOS)

Core Question

Who should have supervised?

Canonical Definition

GOS defines the conditions under which oversight obligations,
supervision structures, and governance review responsibilities
remain identifiable and enforceable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when oversight obligations exist. Ends when oversight
performance can be evaluated.

13. Evidence Accountability Standard (EAS)

Core Question

What do the facts prove?

Canonical Definition

EAS defines the conditions under which evidence remains valid,
reconstructable, reviewable, accountable, and governance-relevant.

Governance Boundary

Begins when evidence exists. Ends when evidentiary conclusions can
be established.

14. Accountability Governance Standard (AGS-A)

Core Question

Who remains answerable?

Canonical Definition

AGS-A defines the conditions under which accountability
relationships remain identifiable, attributable, governable,
and enforceable.

Governance Boundary

Begins when accountability exists. Ends when accountability
ownership can be evaluated.

15. Decision Accountability Standard (DAS)

Core Question

Who made the decision?

Canonical Definition

DAS defines the conditions under which decisions remain attributable
to accountable decision-makers, decision authorities, and
governance structures.

Governance Boundary

Begins when decisions are made. Ends when decision accountability
can be evaluated.

16. Accountability Continuity Standard (ACS)

Core Question

Did accountability remain preserved?

Canonical Definition

ACS defines the conditions under which accountability remains
preserved throughout governance, operational, technological,
contractual, and organizational change.

Governance Boundary

Begins when accountability persists through time. Ends when
accountability continuity can be evaluated.

17. Accountability Chain Standard (ACS-C)

Core Question

How does accountability travel across governance structures?

Canonical Definition

ACS-C defines the conditions under which accountability
relationships remain connected, traceable, reconstructable, and
governable across multi-layer governance structures.

Governance Boundary

Begins when accountability spans multiple governance layers. Ends
when accountability-chain reconstruction becomes possible.

Architectural Position

AGA™ governs the complete accountability lifecycle. The
architecture progresses:

  • Relationship → Authority → Responsibility → Capability → Control → Oversight → Evidence → Accountability →
  • Decision Accountability → Accountability Continuity → Accountability Chain


Together these accountability spaces govern how accountability
remains identifiable, attributable, reconstructable, enforceable,
and governable throughout operational reality.

What AGA™ Defines

AGA™ defines: where accountability begins, where authority operates,
where responsibility exists, where capability becomes relevant,
where control is exercised, where oversight applies, where evidence
becomes relevant, where accountability emerges, where decisions
become accountable, where accountability remains preserved, where
accountability chains operate. AGA™ provides the official
accountability governance language used for accountability space
identification across organizational, institutional, autonomous, and
intelligent systems.

Canonical Principle

AGA™ does not govern what a system does. AGA™ governs how
accountability itself remains identifiable, attributable,
reconstructable, enforceable, and governable throughout
operational reality.

Canonical Closing Statement

AGA™ serves as the official OOF® accountability governance map for
organizations, institutions, governments, autonomous systems, and
Human-AI operational environments. By defining the primary
governable accountability spaces of operational reality, AGA™
provides a structured accountability reference architecture used to
identify, classify, govern, validate, investigate, and understand
accountability throughout the complete accountability lifecycle.
