Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Responsibility Governance Standard (RGS) defines the structural
conditions under which responsibilities remain assignable,
identifiable, traceable, governable, accountable, reconstructable,
enforceable, and operationally valid throughout the
responsibility lifecycle.

RGS governs responsibility.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine who was responsible, what they were responsible for, when
responsibility existed, how responsibility was assigned, whether
responsibility remained valid, and whether responsibility
was fulfilled.

Authority grants the right to act.

Responsibility creates the obligation to act.

RGS governs whether responsibility remains valid.

A. Standard Abstract

Every governance system depends on responsibility.

Organizations assign responsibility.

Institutions assign responsibility.

Governments assign responsibility.

Contractor networks assign responsibility.

AI systems increasingly assign responsibility.

Autonomous agents require responsibility governance.

Human-AI operational environments require responsibility governance.

Without responsibility governance:

  • obligations become unclear
  • responsibility gaps emerge
  • accountability becomes difficult
  • ownership disappears
  • governance failures increase
  • operational reliability decreases


RGS exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Responsibility becomes valid only when responsibility assignments
remain identifiable, traceable, governable, accountable,
reconstructable, enforceable, and operationally valid throughout
the responsibility lifecycle.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Many governance failures originate from responsibility ambiguity.

Common examples include:

  • unclear ownership
  • responsibility conflicts
  • responsibility gaps
  • overlapping responsibilities
  • abandoned responsibilities
  • delegated responsibilities without oversight
  • contractor-chain ambiguity
  • Human-AI responsibility uncertainty


The challenge is not assigning responsibility.

The challenge is preserving responsibility integrity.

RGS exists because responsibility governance has become a
distinct governance space.

E. Responsibility Integrity Logic

Responsibility integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Responsibility Assignment Origin Integrity

Responsibility remains attributable to a legitimate
assignment source.

2. Responsibility Scope Integrity

Responsibility remains connected to clearly defined obligations
and boundaries.

3. Responsibility Validity Integrity

Responsibility remains legitimate, recognized, and
operationally valid.

4. Responsibility Traceability Integrity

Responsibility remains reconstructable throughout the
responsibility lifecycle.

5. Responsibility Accountability Integrity

Responsibility remains connected to accountable governance
structures and consequences.

G. Difference Between Authority and

Responsibility

Authority defines what an actor may do.

Responsibility defines what an actor must do.

Authority grants rights.

Responsibility creates obligations.

Authority may exist without responsibility.

Responsibility may exist without authority.

Both must remain governable.

AGS governs authority.

RGS governs responsibility.

I. Responsibility Failure Rule

Responsibility failure occurs when responsibility assignments cannot
be reliably identified, reconstructed, governed, fulfilled,
enforced, or connected to accountable governance structures.

Examples include:

  • responsibility gaps
  • unclear ownership
  • abandoned responsibilities
  • undocumented responsibility transfers
  • responsibility conflicts
  • contractor-chain ambiguity
  • Human-AI responsibility failures
  • unfulfilled obligations


RGS exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A responsibility-governance environment is valid under RGS when:

  • responsibility assignments remain identifiable
  • responsibility scope remains visible
  • responsibility legitimacy remains demonstrable
  • responsibility history remains reconstructable
  • responsibility accountability remains preservable
  • responsibility fulfillment remains reviewable
  • responsibility governance remains operationally valid


A responsibility-governance environment becomes invalid when:

  • responsibility cannot be identified
  • responsibility scope is unclear
  • responsibility legitimacy disappears
  • responsibility history cannot be reconstructed
  • responsibility accountability is lost
  • responsibility obligations become unverifiable

Canonical Closing Statement

Responsibility Governance Standard (RGS) defines the structural
conditions under which responsibilities remain assignable,
identifiable, traceable, governable, accountable, reconstructable,
enforceable, and operationally valid throughout the
responsibility lifecycle.

Authority may define what actors are allowed to do, but
responsibility defines what actors are obligated to do. Governance
therefore depends not only on authority, but on the ability to
identify, govern, trace, and enforce responsibility
throughout operational reality.
