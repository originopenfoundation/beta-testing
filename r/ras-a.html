Canonical Definition System

Canonical Definition

Responsibility Assignment Standard (RAS-A) defines the structural
conditions under which responsibilities may be assigned, allocated,
transferred, accepted, recorded, governed, reconstructed, and
enforced while preserving legitimacy, traceability, accountability,
clarity, and operational validity throughout
the responsibility-assignment lifecycle.

RAS-A governs responsibility assignment.

The standard establishes the foundational conditions required to
determine who assigned responsibility, to whom responsibility was
assigned, what responsibility was assigned, whether the assignment
was valid, and whether the assigned responsibility
remained governable.

Responsibility does not become operationally valid merely because
a task exists.

Responsibility becomes governable when it is properly assigned.

RAS-A governs that assignment.

A. Standard Abstract

Every governance system assigns responsibility.

Organizations assign responsibility.

Contractors assign responsibility.

Supervisors assign responsibility.

Institutions assign responsibility.

AI orchestration systems increasingly assign responsibility.

Human-AI environments require responsibility assignment governance.

Without responsibility-assignment governance:

  • duties become unclear
  • task ownership becomes fragmented
  • responsibility gaps emerge
  • responsibility conflicts increase
  • accountability becomes weakened
  • operational failures become harder to reconstruct


RAS-A exists to govern these conditions.

B. Core Principle

Responsibility assignment becomes valid only when assigned
responsibilities remain identifiable, legitimate, accepted,
traceable, reconstructable, accountable, and operationally valid
throughout the responsibility- assignment lifecycle.

C. Scope

This standard may apply to:

  • organizations

  • compliance environments
  • AI systems

  • Human-AI operational environments
  • robotics ecosystems

RAS-A applies wherever responsibility is assigned to an actor,
system, role, organization, agent, or operational entity.

D. Why This Standard Exists

Responsibility governance requires more than
identifying responsibility.

Governance must also determine how responsibility was assigned.

Common failures include:

  • unclear task assignment
  • undocumented responsibility allocation
  • assignment to the wrong actor
  • assignment without authority
  • assignment without acceptance
  • assignment without capability
  • overlapping assignments
  • abandoned assignments
  • hidden responsibility transfers


The challenge is not only determining who was responsible.

The challenge is determining whether responsibility was
validly assigned.

RAS-A exists because responsibility assignment is a
distinct governance space.

E. Responsibility Assignment Integrity Logic

Responsibility assignment integrity exists only when the following
remain materially preservable:

1. Assignment Origin Integrity

The assignment remains attributable to a legitimate
assignment source.

2. Assignment Scope Integrity

The assigned responsibility remains connected to clearly defined
duties and boundaries.

3. Assignment Validity Integrity

The assignment remains legitimate, accepted, recognized,
and operationally valid.

4. Assignment Traceability Integrity

The assignment remains reconstructable throughout the
assignment lifecycle.

5. Assignment Accountability Integrity

The assignment remains connected to accountable governance
structures and consequence-bearing relationships.

RAS-A defines the operational architecture space for:

This space exists because responsibility must be assigned before it

RAS-A governs whether responsibility assignment remains valid.

G. Difference Between Responsibility and

Responsibility Assignment

Responsibility defines the duty to act.

Responsibility assignment defines how that duty is allocated to
a specific actor.

Responsibility may exist as an obligation.

Assignment determines who carries that obligation.

RGS governs responsibility.

RAS-A governs responsibility assignment.

Both are required.

I. Responsibility Assignment Failure Rule

Responsibility assignment failure occurs when responsibility
assignments cannot be reliably identified, validated, accepted,
reconstructed, governed, enforced, or connected to
accountable governance structures.

Examples include:

  • undocumented assignment
  • unclear assignment
  • assignment without authority
  • assignment to an incapable actor
  • assignment without acceptance
  • overlapping responsibility assignment
  • hidden responsibility transfer
  • responsibility-assignment gaps


RAS-A exists to expose and govern these conditions.

J. Validity Logic

A responsibility-assignment environment is valid under RAS-A when:

  • assignment sources remain identifiable
  • assigned duties remain clearly defined
  • assignment legitimacy remains demonstrable
  • assignment acceptance remains reviewable
  • assignment history remains reconstructable
  • assignment accountability remains preservable
  • assigned responsibilities remain operationally valid


A responsibility-assignment environment becomes invalid when:

  • assignment origin is unknown
  • assigned duties are unclear
  • assignment authority cannot be demonstrated
  • assignment acceptance is absent or unverifiable
  • assignment history cannot be reconstructed
  • assignment accountability is lost

L. Foundational Principle

Responsibility cannot be enforced if responsibility was
never validly assigned.

Canonical Closing Statement

Responsibility Assignment Standard (RAS-A) defines the structural
conditions under which responsibilities may be assigned, allocated,
transferred, accepted, recorded, governed, reconstructed, and
enforced while preserving legitimacy, traceability, accountability,
clarity, and operational validity throughout
the responsibility-assignment lifecycle.

responsibility assignment cannot be identified, validated, accepted,
or reconstructed, accountability becomes unreliable.
