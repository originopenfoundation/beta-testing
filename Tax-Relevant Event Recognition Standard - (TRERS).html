Canonical Definition

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard (TRERS) is the Parent
Standard within TREGA™ — Tax-Relevant Event Governance
Architecture™ that governs how an observed and sufficiently
contextualized real-world, economic, operational, transactional,
digital, autonomous, machine-generated, or value-affecting
occurrence is evaluated and recognized as a legitimate candidate
for further tax-relevant event governance.

TRERS establishes the governance conditions through which an
occurrence is distinguished from background activity, screened
for potential tax-event relevance, evaluated against governed
recognition criteria, assigned an appropriate recognition
confidence or uncertainty condition, and either advanced,
withheld, rejected, or escalated for further governance.

Recognition under TRERS does not establish that a Tax-Relevant
Event has been fully formed.

It does not establish that the occurrence is legally taxable.

It does not determine jurisdiction, taxpayer status, tax nexus,
tax base, tax rate, liability, reporting obligation, or any other
jurisdiction-specific legal tax consequence.

Its task is narrower and foundational:

to determine whether an occurrence is sufficiently relevant and
sufficiently established to legitimately proceed into
Tax-Relevant Event Formation.

Core Question

When should an observed and contextualized occurrence be
legitimately recognized as a candidate for further tax-relevant
event governance?

Architectural Position

TRERS is the second Parent Standard of TREGA™.

It operates after TRECS — Tax-Relevant Event Context Standard has
established the governed contextual environment surrounding an
occurrence and before TREFS — Tax-Relevant Event Formation
Standard determines how recognized occurrence components are
assembled into a governed event structure.

The architectural transition is:

TRECS establishes the environment necessary to understand the
occurrence.

TRERS determines whether that occurrence warrants progression

TREFS subsequently determines what governed event structure can
legitimately be formed from it.

This separation is fundamental.

Context-Ready ≠ Event-Recognized.

Event-Recognized ≠ Event-Formed.

Event-Recognized ≠ Legally Taxable.

Operational Space

TRERS governs Tax-Relevant Event Recognition.

Its operational space includes: occurrence recognition;
occurrence differentiation; signal-to-occurrence qualification;
potential tax-event relevance screening; recognition criteria;
recognition rules; recognition thresholds where methodologically
appropriate; recognition confidence; recognition uncertainty;
ambiguous occurrence treatment; duplicate recognition risk;
machine-generated occurrence recognition; autonomous occurrence
recognition; distributed occurrence recognition; recognition
escalation; recognition rejection; recognition reconsideration;
recognition traceability.

TRERS establishes whether something observed in governed context
should legitimately become a candidate input to Event Formation.

Operational Scope

TRERS may apply to occurrences originating from: commercial
transactions; financial activity; payments; transfers; production
processes; supply chains; service delivery; digital platforms;
marketplaces; APIs; software systems; digital assets; banking
infrastructure; enterprise systems; autonomous agents; AI
systems; robots; machine-to-machine activity; automated
marketplaces; distributed systems; infrastructure operations;
hybrid physical-digital environments; future machine economies.

TRERS remains actor-neutral and technology-neutral.

An occurrence does not require a human initiator to become
recognizable.

Nor does machine generation make an occurrence tax-relevant by
default.

Occurrence Recognition Principle

Operational systems generate signals continuously.

But:

Signal ≠ Occurrence.

A database update may be a technical signal.

A sensor message may be a technical signal.

An API retry may be a technical signal.

A ledger entry may be a technical signal.

A payment notification may be a technical signal.

TRERS requires that an occurrence be sufficiently distinguishable
from the technical or informational signals through which it
becomes observable.

This prevents taxation infrastructure from treating every
generated record as an independent real-world or economic
occurrence. Occurrence ≠ Tax-Relevant Event

Even where an occurrence is sufficiently established, it has not
yet necessarily become a governed Tax-Relevant Event.

Recognition is an architectural gateway.

It establishes that the occurrence warrants further governance.

Therefore:

Occurrence Recognized

↓

Eligible for Event Formation

not:

Occurrence Recognized

↓

Tax-Relevant Event Fully Established

and never:

Occurrence Recognized

↓

Tax Legally Due.

Recognition Relevance

TRERS determines whether an occurrence has sufficient potential
relevance to the tax-event lifecycle to justify further
governance.

Potential relevance may arise from materially relevant: economic
activity; exchange; transfer; provision of goods or services;
value movement; value change; ownership-related activity;
operational activity; digital activity; platform activity;
autonomous economic execution; machine-to-machine activity;
resource consumption or provision; distributed commercial
activity; other occurrences capable of requiring downstream
taxation evaluation.

Potential relevance is deliberately broader than legal
taxability.

The purpose is to avoid making tax-law conclusions at the
recognition stage.

Recognition Criteria

Recognition should be based upon governed criteria rather than
arbitrary system behavior.

Relevant criteria may consider whether: an occurrence can be
sufficiently distinguished; materially relevant context exists;
the occurrence represents more than technical noise; the
occurrence has plausible economic, operational, transactional,
digital, value-related, or other tax-event relevance; sufficient
information exists for further governance; duplicate
representation risk has been considered; material uncertainty is
identified; the occurrence can be referenced through subsequent
stages.

Recognition criteria may vary by implementation environment while
remaining governed by the methodological boundaries of TRERS.

Recognition Rules

Organizations and systems may implement recognition rules to
identify occurrences requiring TREGA™ processing.

Such rules may be deterministic, probabilistic, AI-assisted,
manually governed, or hybrid.

However:

Recognition Rule ≠ Tax Rule.

A recognition rule determines whether an occurrence should
proceed through event governance.

A tax rule determines a legal taxation consequence under a
jurisdiction.

The two must remain architecturally separated.

Recognition Confidence

Not every occurrence can be recognized with equal certainty.

TRERS therefore permits Recognition Confidence to express the
strength of the governed basis supporting recognition.

Confidence may reflect: source reliability; contextual
completeness; signal consistency; corroborating records;
occurrence distinguishability; recognition-rule strength;
unresolved contradictions.

Recognition Confidence does not represent probability that tax is
legally due.

Therefore:

Recognition Confidence ≠ Taxability Probability.

Recognition Uncertainty

Where evidence or context is insufficient, TRERS must preserve
uncertainty rather than manufacture recognition certainty.

An occurrence may remain:

Recognition Unresolved

where the available information cannot legitimately support
either recognition or rejection.

This is particularly important in fragmented, distributed,
autonomous, or cross-border systems where complete information
may not be immediately available.

Recognition States

A TREGA™ implementation may represent recognition through
governance states such as:

1. Occurrence Observed 2. Occurrence Under Recognition 3.
Recognition Unresolved 4. Occurrence Recognized 5. Occurrence Not
Recognized 6. Recognition Escalated 7. Recognition Reconsidered

These states represent governance status.

They do not represent legal tax status.

Recognition Rejection

TRERS must permit an occurrence to be rejected from further event
governance where it does not satisfy legitimate recognition
conditions.

Examples may include: technical noise; duplicate signals; system
retries; internal telemetry without relevant economic meaning;
corrupted records; irrelevant operational activity;
insufficiently established occurrences.

Rejection prevents unnecessary Event Formation.

However:

Recognition Rejection ≠ Legal Non-Taxability Determination.

It means only that the occurrence, as currently represented, does
not legitimately proceed through TREGA™ recognition.

Duplicate Recognition Risk

One real-world activity may generate many records.

For example:

Order Created

↓

API Call

↓

Payment Authorization

↓

Payment
Confirmation

↓

ERP Entry

↓

Warehouse Signal

↓

Invoice Record

TRERS must avoid assuming that every observable record represents
a separate occurrence requiring independent Event Formation.

Conversely, several genuinely distinct occurrences must not be
collapsed merely because they share identifiers or systems.

Therefore:

Multiple Records ≠ Multiple Occurrences.

and:

Shared Identifier ≠ Single Occurrence by Default.

Machine-Generated Recognition

Machine environments create recognition challenges because they
can generate operational records at volumes far beyond human
economic activity.

An autonomous system may produce thousands or millions of: API
calls; state transitions; resource allocations; machine
instructions; settlement messages; automated purchases; service
invocations.

TRERS must distinguish technical execution activity from
occurrences that plausibly require further tax-event governance.

Therefore:

Machine Activity ≠ Tax-Relevant Occurrence by Default.

Autonomous Recognition

An occurrence may originate without immediate human intervention.

TREGA™ must therefore permit recognition of activity initiated
by: AI agents; autonomous software; robots; automated
marketplaces; autonomous infrastructure; machine coordination
systems.

TRERS does not determine legal personhood, identity,
responsibility, taxpayer status, or liability of those systems.

It determines only whether an occurrence produced through such an
environment warrants further event governance.

Distributed Occurrence Recognition

One occurrence may become visible through several systems or
locations.

No single record may contain the complete representation.

TRERS must therefore support recognition where evidence of an
occurrence is distributed across:

Platform A

↓

Payment System B

↓

Infrastructure C

↓

Enterprise System D

Recognition may legitimately depend upon governed combination of
contextualized signals.

However:

Distributed Evidence ≠ Multiple Events.

Recognition Escalation

Some occurrences cannot be legitimately recognized or rejected
automatically.

TRERS must permit escalation where: evidence conflicts;
contextual ambiguity remains material; recognition criteria
produce inconsistent results; machine-generated activity cannot
be reliably interpreted; duplicate occurrence risk cannot be
resolved; the potential significance of incorrect recognition is
material.

Escalation may involve additional evidence, another governance
mechanism, human review, validation, audit, or another
appropriate process.

Escalation exists to preserve methodological legitimacy under
uncertainty.

Recognition Reconsideration

Recognition may later require reconsideration where: new evidence
becomes available; context is corrected; duplicate records are
discovered; a previously unresolved occurrence becomes
sufficiently established; the original recognition basis is shown
to be materially incorrect.

Reconsideration must remain traceable.

A changed recognition outcome should not silently erase the
earlier governance state.

Recognition Traceability

A recognized occurrence should remain traceable to the basis upon
which recognition occurred.

Where materially required, the system should preserve: relevant
context; recognition criteria; recognition rule or method;
supporting evidence references; uncertainty; confidence;
escalation; reconsideration.

This allows later stages to understand why the occurrence entered
Event Formation.

Recognition Independence from Tax Rules

TRERS must remain capable of recognizing an occurrence
independently of the jurisdiction-specific tax rule eventually
applied to it.

This is essential because the same governed occurrence may later
be evaluated by different taxation systems.

The recognition layer should not have to reconstruct operational
reality whenever tax legislation changes.

Therefore:

Tax Rule Change ≠ Occurrence Recognition Change by Default.

Jurisdiction-Neutral Recognition

An occurrence may have characteristics relevant to several
jurisdictions.

TRERS may preserve those characteristics.

It does not decide which jurisdiction has legal authority to tax.

Therefore:

Cross-Border Recognition ≠ Jurisdiction Determination.

A single recognized occurrence may later support multiple
legitimate jurisdictional evaluations.

Recognition Readiness

An occurrence becomes recognition-ready when sufficient context
and information exist to evaluate whether it should proceed
through TRERS.

Recognition readiness does not itself establish recognition.

Therefore:

Recognition-Ready ≠ Recognized.

Similarly:

Recognized ≠ Formed.

and:

Formed ≠ Taxable.

These distinctions preserve the sequential integrity of TREGA™.

Governance Boundary with TRECS

TRECS — Tax-Relevant Event Context Standard governs the
environment necessary to understand the occurrence.

TRERS consumes that governed context.

TRECS asks:

What surrounding conditions are necessary to understand what
happened?

TRERS asks:

Does the contextualized occurrence legitimately warrant further
tax-relevant event governance?

Therefore:

Event Context ≠ Event Recognition.

Governance Boundary with TREFS

TREFS — Tax-Relevant Event Formation Standard begins after
legitimate recognition.

TRERS does not determine the complete components, root
association, dependencies, or structural composition of the
governed event.

TRERS determines whether Event Formation should begin.

Therefore:

Recognition ≠ Formation.

Governance Boundary with TREDS

TREDS — Tax-Relevant Event Determination Standard later
determines whether the fully governed structure is sufficiently
established as a Tax-Relevant Event suitable for downstream
taxation analysis.

TRERS performs a much earlier gateway function.

Therefore:

Event Recognition ≠ Event Determination.

Recognition means:

this occurrence warrants further governance.

Determination may later establish:

a sufficiently governed Tax-Relevant Event exists.

Neither statement means:

tax is legally due.

Cross-Architecture Boundary

TRERS may consume governed results from other OOF® architectures
where available.

ORA™ may establish what actually occurred operationally.

ASGA™ may govern autonomous-system operation and execution.

OBIDENITY® may govern identity, origin, ownership, authority,
permission, and provenance.

VFM® may govern what happened to value.

CLA® may provide governed classification.

INTEGROS® may govern integrity.

ADIT® may govern evidence and auditability.

VALIDOS™ may validate relevant claims, methods, or results.

AGA™ may govern accountability.

TRERS does not absorb these governed spaces.

Its responsibility remains:

recognition of the contextualized occurrence for progression
through TREGA™.

Canonical Closing Statement

Tax-Relevant Event Recognition Standard establishes the
recognition governance layer of TREGA™ — Tax-Relevant Event
Governance Architecture™ by determining when a sufficiently
contextualized real-world, economic, operational, transactional,
digital, autonomous, machine-generated, or value-affecting
occurrence legitimately warrants progression into Tax-Relevant
Event Formation. Through governance of occurrence recognition,
relevance screening, recognition criteria, recognition rules,
confidence, uncertainty, duplicate-recognition risk,
machine-generated activity, distributed occurrences, escalation,
rejection, reconsideration, and traceability, TRERS prevents raw
signals, technical records, system retries, platform activity,
autonomous execution, or isolated transactional data from being
automatically elevated into governed Tax-Relevant Events. The
Standard preserves the architectural distinctions between Signal
and Occurrence, Context and Recognition, Recognition and
Formation, Recognition and Event Determination, potential tax
relevance and legal taxability, and cross-border occurrence
characteristics and Tax Jurisdiction. By creating a
jurisdiction-neutral recognition gateway between governed
operational context and formal Event Formation, TRERS enables
human, enterprise, digital, and autonomous economic activity to
enter the TREGA™ lifecycle through a consistent methodological
process without allowing recognition itself to determine whether
tax is legally due.
