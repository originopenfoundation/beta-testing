Correlation Uncertainty

Canonical Definition

Correlation Uncertainty Module (SCUM) defines the governance conditions under
which uncertainty affecting the relationship between simulation and
Operational Reality is identified, attributed where supportable, classified,
bounded, propagated, documented, communicated, and preserved throughout
Simulation-to-Reality Correlation.

SCUM governs uncertainty concerning:

how confidently a simulation can be said to correspond to reality within a
defined object, condition, scenario, environment, time, comparison basis, and
correlation scope.

Its purpose is not to eliminate uncertainty.

Its purpose is to prevent uncertainty from disappearing when simulation
results are compared with reality, aligned against observations, or
subsequently used as evidence.

Operational Role

SCUM is the fifth and final internal governance space of the
Simulation-to-Reality Correlation Standard (SRCS).

The preceding modules establish:

SRCM — What simulation and reality observations may legitimately be compared?

SCCM — According to which criteria should correspondence be assessed?

SDMM — Where and how materially do simulation and reality differ?

SRAM — Which deviations require alignment, and how should that alignment
occur?

SCUM asks:

How certain are we about the resulting Simulation-to-Reality Correlation, what
remains unknown, and where does that uncertainty limit interpretation or
downstream reliance?

Module Operational Space

SCUM governs: correlation uncertainty identification, uncertainty source
identification, uncertainty classification, uncertainty attribution,
uncertainty magnitude, uncertainty bounds, measurement uncertainty,
observational uncertainty, representation uncertainty, model uncertainty,
assumption-related uncertainty, environmental uncertainty, input uncertainty

status, and downstream uncertainty transfer.

Module Function

SCUM prevents a correlation statement such as:

“The simulation matches reality.”

from being treated as complete unless the uncertainty surrounding that
statement is also visible.

A governed correlation statement should instead be capable of expressing:

what corresponds,

under which conditions,

against which reality basis,

with what observed deviations,

with what remaining uncertainty,

and:

where that uncertainty becomes material.

Correlation Is Not Certainty

SIMULOS® establishes:

Observed correlation does not eliminate uncertainty about the
simulation-reality relationship.

Strong correspondence may coexist with uncertainty concerning: unobserved
conditions, rare events, measurement limitations, model structure,
environmental variability, future conditions, human behavior, autonomous
behavior, interaction effects, or incomplete Operational Reality knowledge.

Uncertainty Status Uncertainty Source

SCUM should identify the origin of uncertainty where evidence permits.

Potential sources include: incomplete Operational Reality observations,
measurement error, sensor limitations, sparse data, uncertain Reality
Representation, uncertain assumptions, model simplification, unknown
environmental conditions, input variability, scenario incompleteness,
stochastic behavior, execution variability, incomplete comparison, uncertain
correlation criteria, deviation measurement limitations, alignment effects, or
unknown causes.

Unknown Source Must Remain Unknown

SIMULOS® establishes:

Uncertainty must not be assigned a cause merely because governance requires a
classification.

Where its source cannot be sufficiently established:

Uncertainty Source Undetermined

is a legitimate governed state.

Epistemic Uncertainty

Epistemic Uncertainty arises from incomplete knowledge.

Examples include: insufficient observations, incomplete model knowledge,
uncertain parameter values, poorly understood interactions, limited
environmental knowledge, and incomplete causal understanding.

Epistemic uncertainty may potentially be reduced through additional:

evidence, measurement, modeling, experimentation, or observation.

Aleatory Uncertainty

Aleatory Uncertainty arises from inherent variability or stochastic behavior
in the represented reality.

It may not be eliminable simply by obtaining more information.

SCUM distinguishes:

uncertainty caused by what we do not know

from:

uncertainty caused by reality itself being variable.

Unknown Uncertainty

Some uncertainty cannot yet be meaningfully classified as epistemic or
aleatory.

SCUM preserves:

Unknown / Unclassified Correlation
Uncertainty

rather than forcing artificial certainty about the nature of uncertainty
itself.

Reality-Reference Uncertainty

Operational Reality is not always perfectly observable.

Reality observations may themselves contain: measurement uncertainty, sampling
limitations, missing states, incomplete telemetry, human reporting error,
sensor limitations, temporal gaps, or inaccessible conditions.

Therefore:

Reality Observation ≠ Perfect Reality Knowledge.

Simulation Uncertainty and Reality
Uncertainty Must Remain Distinguishable

A simulation-reality difference may arise from:

Simulation Uncertainty

Reality Observation Uncertainty

or:

Both.

SCUM should preserve this distinction where possible.

Comparison Uncertainty

Even where both simulation and reality observations are individually strong,
uncertainty may exist in whether they are sufficiently comparable.

Possible causes include: different environmental conditions, different timing,
different configurations, different measurement resolution, different object
states, or incomplete contextual equivalence.

Criteria Uncertainty

Correlation Criteria may themselves contain uncertainty.

For example, an organization may not know whether:

±2%

or:

±5%

represents a materially sufficient correspondence boundary for a particular
operational purpose.

SCUM preserves uncertainty in the criterion rather than hiding it inside the
correlation result.

Deviation Uncertainty

An observed deviation may have uncertainty around: magnitude, direction,
frequency, localization, cause, propagation, or materiality.

Therefore:

Deviation Record

and:

Deviation Uncertainty

should remain distinguishable.

Alignment Uncertainty

A Reality Alignment action may improve observed correlation while creating
uncertainty about: generalization, unobserved scenarios, model interactions,
new parameter regions, or unintended downstream effects.

Alignment therefore does not automatically reduce total uncertainty.

Residual Correlation Uncertainty

After comparison, deviation assessment, and alignment, uncertainty may remain.

This becomes:

Residual Correlation Uncertainty.

Residual uncertainty should be explicitly preserved rather than implicitly
converted into confidence.

Uncertainty Magnitude

Where evidence permits, uncertainty may be expressed quantitatively through:
confidence intervals, probability distributions, error ranges, tolerance
intervals, sensitivity ranges, variance, credible intervals, or other
appropriate methods.

Where numerical quantification is not supportable, structured qualitative
classification may be used.

No False Precision Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Correlation uncertainty must not be expressed more precisely than the
available evidence permits.

A numerical percentage can create misleading authority where the underlying
evidence cannot support that precision.

Qualitative Uncertainty Classification

Where quantitative measurement is inappropriate, implementation may use
classifications such as:

Low

Moderate

High

Critical

or:

Undetermined.

These classifications must remain bounded to the applicable correlation
context.

Uncertainty Bounds

Where possible, SCUM should establish the range within which the correlation
relationship is reasonably supported.

For example:

Strong Correlation — Nominal Conditions

Moderate Uncertainty — High Temperature

High Uncertainty — Extreme Combined
Conditions

Unknown — Unobserved Failure Configuration

This is more informative than a single global confidence score.

Condition-Specific Uncertainty

Correlation uncertainty may vary significantly across conditions.

Therefore:

Correlation Confidence is a surface, not necessarily a single number.

A simulation may be highly correlated with reality in one operational region
and poorly understood in another.

Scenario-Specific Uncertainty

Each materially relevant scenario may possess a different uncertainty profile.

This is particularly important for: edge cases, stress conditions, rare
events, novel environments, and combinations never directly observed in
Operational Reality.

Temporal Uncertainty

A simulation may correspond well with current reality while uncertainty
increases over time because: the environment changes, system behavior evolves,
components degrade, software changes, populations change, or Operational
Reality drifts.

SCUM therefore allows correlation uncertainty to be time-dependent.

Spatial Uncertainty

Correlation may be strong in observed geographic or physical regions while
remaining uncertain elsewhere.

This matters for: autonomous vehicles, robotics, climate simulation,
aerospace, infrastructure, and geographically distributed systems.

Behavioral Uncertainty

Behavioral uncertainty concerns uncertainty about whether simulated actors or
systems behave sufficiently like their real counterparts.

This can be particularly material for: humans, AI agents, autonomous systems,
multi-agent systems, adaptive systems, and adversarial environments.

Interaction Uncertainty

Even where individual components are well represented, their interactions may
remain uncertain.

SCUM therefore governs uncertainty arising from:

component × component

human × system

agent × agent

system × environment

and other interaction relationships.

Uncertainty Dependency

Uncertainties may depend upon one another.

For example:

SCUM should preserve material dependencies where identifiable.

Uncertainty Interaction

Multiple individually modest uncertainties may interact to produce a
materially larger uncertainty.

For example:

Sensor Noise Weather Variability Timing
Uncertainty

may create:

High Autonomous Behavior Correlation Uncertainty.

Uncertainty Propagation

Correlation uncertainty may propagate downstream through the simulation

No Uncertainty Laundering Principle

SIMULOS® establishes:

Uncertainty identified within Simulation-to-Reality Correlation must not
disappear merely because the simulation output enters another governance
process.

If VALIDOS® uses the simulation as validation evidence, the relevant
uncertainty must travel with it.

Uncertainty Accumulation

Where multiple uncertain relationships contribute to one correlation
conclusion, SCUM should determine whether uncertainty accumulates materially.

A simulation should not claim high overall certainty merely because each
individual uncertainty appears small when assessed separately.

Uncertainty Reduction

Where uncertainty is reducible, SCUM may identify actions such as: additional
Operational Reality observations, additional measurements, broader scenario
execution, targeted experiments, higher-fidelity representation, model
refinement, parameter reassessment, additional comparison, or repeated
execution.

Reducible vs Irreducible Uncertainty

SCUM distinguishes:

Reducible Uncertainty

from:

Currently Irreducible Uncertainty.

This matters because governance responses differ.

Reducible uncertainty may create an evidence or simulation improvement
requirement.

Irreducible uncertainty may instead require:

bounded interpretation, explicit conditions, or restricted reliance.

Uncertainty Reduction Has a Cost

Reducing uncertainty may require: computation, testing, physical experiments,
additional missions, new sensors, more data, time, or operational exposure.

SIMULOS® does not require uncertainty to be reduced indefinitely.

The appropriate reduction depth depends upon the simulation purpose and
downstream consequence.

Material Uncertainty

Uncertainty becomes material when it can materially affect: interpretation,
correlation classification, simulation output meaning, validation, safety
assessment, operational decision-making, autonomous-system behavior, mission
planning, or reliance.

Uncertainty Threshold

Where appropriate, implementations may establish thresholds beyond which:

additional evidence,

additional simulation,

alignment,

validation restriction,

or:

reliance limitation

is required.

SIMULOS® does not prescribe one universal uncertainty threshold.

Correlation Confidence

An implementation may derive a bounded Correlation Confidence representation
from the governed uncertainty basis.

However:

Correlation Confidence must never conceal the conditions, scope, or unresolved
uncertainty upon which it depends. Correlation Confidence ≠ Probability of
Reality

A confidence representation must not imply that the simulation itself has
become reality or that all future real-world behavior has been
probabilistically captured.

Correlation Coverage and Uncertainty

High correlation inside a narrow observed region does not establish low
uncertainty outside that region.

Therefore:

Observed Correlation Coverage must remain distinguishable from Unobserved
Correlation Uncertainty.

Unknown Regions

Where no sufficient Operational Reality basis exists, SCUM should permit
explicit classification such as:

Correlation Unknown — Insufficient Reality Observation.

This is especially important where simulation exists precisely because direct
real-world testing is difficult or impossible.

Novel Conditions

A simulation may be used to explore conditions that have never occurred or
cannot safely be reproduced.

SCUM does not prohibit this.

Instead, it requires the distinction between:

Empirically Correlated Region

and:

Simulation-Only / Uncorrelated Region.

Extrapolation Uncertainty

Where simulation extends beyond observed reality, SCUM should identify:

Extrapolation Uncertainty.

The farther the simulation moves beyond the governed correlation basis, the
more carefully its correspondence claims should be bounded.

No Correlation Transfer by Similarity

SIMULOS® establishes:

Correlation established for one condition, environment, configuration, or
system version must not automatically transfer to another merely because the
two appear similar.

Transfer requires a governed basis.

Cross-Version Reality Correlation

A new system or simulation version may inherit some correlation evidence from
a previous version only where the relevant relationship remains materially
comparable.

Otherwise new correlation assessment is required.

Correlation Uncertainty and Decision
Consequence

The same uncertainty level may be acceptable for:

early design exploration

but unacceptable for:

safety-critical deployment authorization.

SCUM therefore preserves downstream consequence context without itself making
the final reliance decision.

Uncertainty Communication

Material uncertainty should be communicated in a form usable by: engineers,
decision-makers, validators, auditors, regulators, autonomous systems, and
downstream AI systems.

The communication should preserve both:

what is known

and:

what remains uncertain.

Relationship to SRCM

SRCM determines whether simulation and reality observations are sufficiently
comparable.

SCUM preserves uncertainty remaining in that comparison basis.

Relationship to SCCM

SCCM establishes the criteria for judging correspondence.

SCUM governs uncertainty affecting the interpretation and application of those
criteria.

Relationship to SDMM

SDMM governs observed Simulation-Reality Deviations.

SCUM governs uncertainty concerning those deviations and the broader
correlation relationship.

Therefore:

Deviation ≠ Uncertainty.

A deviation is an observed difference.

Uncertainty concerns how confidently that difference and the resulting
correlation relationship are understood.

Relationship to SRAM

SRAM governs Reality Alignment.

SCUM determines what uncertainty remains before, during, and after alignment.

Therefore:

Alignment may reduce deviation without eliminating uncertainty.

Relationship to ORA™

ORA™ governs Operational Reality.

SCUM does not assign certainty to Operational Reality itself.

It governs how uncertainty in the available reality basis affects the
simulation-to-reality relationship.

Relationship to VALIDOS®

VALIDOS® may use simulation and Simulation-to-Reality Correlation as part of a
Validation Evidence basis.

SCUM ensures that material correlation uncertainty remains attached to that
evidence.

Therefore:

VALIDOS® must not receive simulation correlation as stronger evidence than
SIMULOS® has actually established.

Relationship to INTEGROS®

INTEGROS® may govern integrity properties of: observations, measurements,
datasets, records, and uncertainty artifacts.

SCUM governs the simulation-specific uncertainty relationship.

Relationship to SOGS

SOGS governs what simulation outputs are and what may legitimately be inferred
from them.

SCUM provides a correlation-uncertainty basis that may materially constrain
those inferences where outputs are interpreted as representations of reality.

Relationship to Operational Reliance

SCUM does not itself authorize operational reliance.

It provides the uncertainty basis required by downstream governance to
determine whether reliance is legitimate.

Therefore:

Correlation Uncertainty → Reliance Input

not:

Correlation Uncertainty → Reliance Authorization.

Autonomous Systems Application

An autonomous system may show excellent simulation-reality correlation across
millions of nominal simulation events.

However, limited real-world observations may exist for: rare pedestrian
behavior, unusual weather combinations, degraded sensors, adversarial
interactions, infrastructure failures, multi-agent conflicts, or emergency
recovery.

SCUM prevents nominal correlation from being generalized automatically into
low uncertainty across the entire autonomous operating domain.

A machine-readable Correlation Uncertainty Map can identify where the system's
simulation evidence is strong and where real-world correspondence remains
uncertain.

Space and Mission Simulation Application

Space agencies frequently rely on simulation precisely where direct reality
evidence is sparse.

A spacecraft or planetary-landing simulation may possess strong correlation
evidence for: subsystem tests, terrestrial hardware testing, known orbital
conditions, previous mission telemetry, and observed environmental parameters.

But uncertainty may remain high for: never-observed surface interactions,
extreme radiation combinations, long-duration degradation, unknown atmospheric
behavior, rare subsystem combinations, autonomous recovery, or first-of-kind
mission conditions.

SCUM allows those regions to remain explicitly:

Simulation-Supported but
Reality-Correlation Uncertain

rather than silently inheriting the confidence of well-tested regions.

This can reveal exactly where additional testing, observation,
instrumentation, scenario coverage, or conservative mission constraints
provide the greatest value.

Minimum Implementation Framework

1. Establish the Correlation Basis

Identify the simulation, Operational Reality reference, comparison basis,
criteria, deviations, and alignment state. 2. Identify Material Uncertainty

Determine where the correlation relationship remains uncertain. 3. Identify
the Uncertainty Source

Attribute uncertainty where evidence permits.

Otherwise record:

Source Undetermined. 4. Classify the
Uncertainty

Determine whether uncertainty is: epistemic, aleatory, mixed, or unknown.

5. Localize the Uncertainty

Identify the affected: condition, scenario, component, environment, behavior,
interaction, time, or output.

6. Bound or Quantify Where Supportable

Use numerical or qualitative bounds appropriate to the evidence. 7. Map
Dependencies and Interaction

Determine whether uncertainties depend upon or amplify one another. 8. Assess
Propagation

Identify where uncertainty can affect downstream interpretation or governance.
9. Determine Materiality

Assess whether the uncertainty materially affects the intended simulation
purpose or downstream use. 10. Determine Reducibility

Identify whether additional evidence or simulation activity can materially
reduce the uncertainty. 11. Preserve Residual Uncertainty

Record what remains unresolved after available reduction or alignment actions.
12. Establish Correlation Uncertainty Status

Assign a bounded status appropriate to the applicable correlation scope. 13.
Transfer Material Uncertainty Downstream

Ensure that simulation outputs, validation processes, audits, or reliance
decisions receive the relevant uncertainty information. 14. Preserve
Traceability

Maintain the relationship:

Reality Basis → Comparison → Criteria → Deviation → Alignment → Uncertainty →
Downstream Use.

Use Case 1 — Autonomous Driving Beyond
Tested Conditions

An autonomous driving simulation demonstrates strong correlation with
Operational Reality across extensive urban driving tests.

However, only limited real-world evidence exists for:

heavy snow + partially blocked sensors + unusual pedestrian movement + low
visibility.

SCUM prevents the strong nominal correlation from automatically extending into
this region.

The system records:

Nominal Urban Conditions → Correlation
Uncertainty Low

Heavy Snow → Correlation Uncertainty
Moderate

Heavy Snow + Sensor Degradation + Rare Pedestrian Interaction → Correlation
Uncertainty High

This uncertainty can then travel with the simulation evidence into validation
and operational decision-making. Use Case 2 — First-of-Kind Space Mission

A space agency uses simulation to assess an autonomous landing sequence for a
destination where direct Operational Reality observations are necessarily
limited.

The simulation correlates strongly with: terrestrial hardware testing,
propulsion tests, sensor testing, and comparable prior mission data.

However, the exact combination of:

surface properties + environmental conditions + autonomous response + landing
dynamics

has never been directly observed.

SCUM prevents correlation from known regions from being silently transferred
into the unknown mission region.

The resulting correlation basis may state:

Subsystem Correlation — Strong

Known Environmental Range — Sufficiently
Bounded

Exact Landing Environment — Material
Epistemic Uncertainty

Combined First-of-Kind Landing Interaction — Correlation Unknown /
Simulation-Supported

This gives engineers and decision-makers a governed map of where additional
evidence can reduce uncertainty and where uncertainty must instead remain an
explicit mission constraint.

Architectural Position

SCUM is the fifth and final module of the Simulation-to-Reality Correlation
Standard (SRCS).

The complete SRCS progression is:

SRCM — Simulation-Reality Comparison

↓

SCCM — Simulation Correlation Criteria

↓

SDMM — Simulation-Reality Deviation

↓

SRAM — Reality Alignment

↓

SCUM — Correlation Uncertainty

Together, these five modules answer:

What can be compared?

↓

According to what criteria?

↓

Where does simulation differ from reality?

↓

What should be aligned?

↓

How certain are we about the resulting relationship?

This closes the governed Simulation-to-Reality Correlation space without
converting correlation into validation or simulation into reality.

Foundational Principle

Simulation similarity is not reality equivalence, and correlation confidence
must never exceed the reality evidence, comparison basis, observed conditions,
and unresolved uncertainty that actually support it.

Canonical Closing Statement

Correlation Uncertainty Module (SCUM) completes the Simulation-to-Reality
Correlation Standard by governing what remains uncertain after simulation and
Operational Reality have been compared, deviations identified, and justified
alignment performed. By distinguishing simulation uncertainty from
reality-reference uncertainty, epistemic uncertainty from aleatory
variability, observed correlation from unobserved regions, deviation from
uncertainty, alignment from certainty, and empirical correspondence from
extrapolation, SCUM prevents strong correlation in known conditions from
becoming an unsupported claim about reality as a whole. Through uncertainty
identification, classification, bounding, dependency mapping, propagation,
reduction, residual-uncertainty preservation, machine-readable disclosure, and
downstream transfer, SCUM ensures that every Simulation-to-Reality Correlation
remains only as certain, broad, and operationally meaningful as the evidence
actually permits.
